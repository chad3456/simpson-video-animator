# LUMEN E7: "Letters to Madina"

*In 1942 a pro-Congress Urdu paper in the small town of Bijnor asked its readers what they understood by Pakistan, and printed the answers. Episode 7 follows that debate: a critic who said mass migration was impossible, a defender who promised hostages on both sides, readers from Bombay to Raichur, and letters to Jinnah asking what Pakistan would mean for those left behind.*

Source: Venkat Dhulipala, Creating a New Medina: State Power, Islam, and the Quest for Pakistan in Late Colonial North India (Cambridge University Press, 2015), chapter 6; the Madina debate and letters in the Quaid-i-Azam Papers as quoted there.

- `0:05` In May 1944 a schoolboy in the small town of Amroha sat down to write to the most famous Muslim in India. Jinnah had written to him once, and he wanted to write again.
- `0:18` His problem was at home. His father was a Congressman, a keen one, who mocked the League at the dinner table. And the boy admitted something remarkable: he did not even know what Pakistan was. Would the Qaid please answer his father's objections, so that he could win him over?
- `0:34` Four years after Lahore, that was the question in a thousand homes. What, exactly, is Pakistan? One newspaper decided to let its readers argue it out.

## [0:54] Chapter One: A Paper Called Madina


_[0:59] Scene `paper`: void / ink; on screen: cite, paper, typer._

- `0:59` The Madina was printed in Bijnor, a quiet district town in western U.P. Founded in 1913, it had carried verse by Iqbal and Akbar Ilahabadi, been banned in Punjab after the massacre at Amritsar, and stood for three decades as the most prominent pro-Congress Urdu paper in north India.
- `1:19` In the autumn of 1942, with Congress leaders in jail after Quit India and the argument over Pakistan at its height, the editor did something unusual. He asked readers what they understood by Pakistan, and whether it would help or harm India's Muslims. Then, despite wartime paper rationing, he printed their answers.

_[1:40] Scene `letters`: void / ink; on screen: cite, panel, samap, typer._

- `1:40` Letters came from all over U.P., and from far beyond it: from Bombay on the west coast, from Chatgaon, that is Chittagong, in eastern Bengal, and from Raichur in the Deccan.
- `1:51` The debate settled into two long essays, one against Pakistan and one for it, printed in instalments over several months, with readers' replies in between. Dhulipala argues that they show us something rare: not the leaders' strategies, but what educated small-town Muslims actually thought.

## [2:10] Chapter Two: The Critic


_[2:14] Scene `bazmi`: void / ink; on screen: cite, dossier, typer._

- `2:14` The case against was made by a writer named Abu Syed Bazmi, freshly released from a jail in one of the princely states. League workers were marching through U.P. shouting Pakistan, and only Pakistan. Bazmi asked a sly question: hadn't Congress already given it to them?
- `2:32` The Congress president, Maulana Azad, had written that no province would be forced to stay in the Indian Union. Any province could leave, Bengal included, which was more than the British Cripps offer allowed. So why was the League still shouting?
- `2:47` Then he turned to money. Lose the eastern districts of Punjab, and Calcutta with the coal and iron around it, and Pakistan would be poor. Modern wars, he wrote, were fought with coal, steel, rubber and factories. The strong men of Punjab could not win them with their arms alone.

_[3:04] Scene `rocks`: void / ink; on screen: cite, stat, typer._

- `3:04` He saved his fiercest words for the idea of moving people. To create safe Muslim majorities, he calculated, perhaps thirty million Muslims would have to move in and as many Hindus move out. The greatest migration the world had seen, Greeks leaving Turkey, involved about a million people, and that was called a miracle. Who would pay? Who would buy the land? And Hindus, he wrote, were not rocks and wood to be sorted and slotted at will.
- `3:28` The Ganga and the Jamuna were as holy to Hindus as the Hijaz to Muslims. They would not give up those lands.
- `3:35` He reminded readers of 1920, when thousands of Indian Muslims had migrated to Afghanistan in religious fervour, only to be driven back across the border in misery. Five years later, the partition that Bazmi called impossible would uproot millions.

## [3:53] Chapter Three: Letters to the Editor


_[3:58] Scene `replies`: room / archive; on screen: cite, doc._

- `3:58` The replies were vivid. From Bombay, a Hakim named Mohammad Anwar insisted Pakistan would be rich. Chittagong could be built into a port to rival Calcutta. Coal and iron could simply be bought from Hindustan. And the frontier? The very word jihad, he wrote, would do the work of an entire army.
- `4:16` From Raichur, Mohammad Abid Sharif worried about something else. Congress schools were teaching boys and girls together, and Muslim girls were singing and dancing on stage. Did U.P.'s Muslims want to drag the Muslims of Punjab and Bengal down with them? As for those left behind, retaliation would protect them.
- `4:36` From Aligarh, a student named Adil Mirza, with a B.A. and a B.Com., was scathing. Ports cost crores, he wrote. Buy coal from Hindustan? That was Marie Antoinette: let them eat cake. Nobody doubted an Islamic government was good. The question was whether it was possible. The League's Pakistan, he said, was Shaikh Chilli's paradise, the daydreamer's heaven that everyone loves and no one can find the road to.

## [4:59] Chapter Four: The Defence


_[5:04] Scene `amrohvi`: void / ink; on screen: cite, dossier, typer._

- `5:04` Five weeks later the paper began the reply, by a writer from Amroha who signed himself Abul Nazar Rizvi Amrohvi. Azad's offer, he argued, was a trap. In a provincial vote, Hindus and Sikhs, organised and united, would vote too, and Punjab and Bengal would never leave. The right to secede had to belong to Muslims as a community, not to provinces.
- `5:26` Pakistan would be poor? England, he answered, was once the Baluchistan of Europe. Machines decide wars? China had not surrendered to Japan. Freedom, he wrote, rests on the passion for it.
- `5:38` And Pakistan would be Islamic, by his definition, if its ruler or its majority was Muslim. Turkey counted, though it had abolished Islamic law. Dhulipala calls him a modernist, who did not picture an Islamic state at all.

_[5:53] Scene `fate`: void / smoke; on screen: cite, cquote, typer._

- `5:53` On the hardest question, the Muslims of U.P., he was frank. Under Pakistan they would certainly live under Hindu rule. Neither you nor anyone else, he wrote, can change their fate. Punjabis and Bengalis might not always come to their aid.
- `6:08` They should make the sacrifice, as they had for the Caliphate and for Turkey. And they would not suffer alone. Their subjection, he promised, would be matched by the subjection of crores of Hindus in Pakistan.
- `6:21` Even Pakistan's most modern-minded defender ended up in the same place as the weavers of Tanda.

## [6:28] Chapter Five: Dear Qaid-e-Azam


_[6:33] Scene `qaid`: room / archive; on screen: cite, doc._

- `6:33` Even the League's own officials were unsure. In July 1944 the secretary of the Lucknow district League wrote to Jinnah: so far, no one has been able to give a definite conception of Pakistan. So he offered his own. Hindus in Pakistan would have civic rights but no political rights. They would be treated as aliens, and Muslims outside would be treated the same way, until, over time, everyone migrated home.
- `6:57` And the boy from Amroha set down his father's sharpest objection. U.P. was eighty-six per cent Hindu. The League's answer was retaliation, but it was far harder to crush the Hindus of Punjab and Bengal than the Muslims of U.P. Under what rules, he asked Jinnah, can we be saved from crushing?
- `7:15` There is no reply in the file.

## [7:18] Chapter Six: What the Historians Say


_[7:23] Scene `tests`: void / smoke; on screen: verdict._

- `7:23` So what holds up? That ordinary Muslims argued over Pakistan in detail: well supported. The Madina's pages are the evidence.
- `7:32` That Pakistan's supporters shared one clear picture of it: weak. Here Dhulipala's own sources partly support his critics. A Hakim in Bombay, a modernist in Amroha and a League secretary in Lucknow each imagined a different country.
- `7:47` That moving tens of millions of people could never happen: wrong, tragically. In 1947 it happened, without plan or protection.
- `7:57` And that losing Calcutta, its port and its coal would leave the east poor and resentful: partly borne out. East Pakistan built up Chittagong, but economic grievances against the west fed the war of 1971 that created Bangladesh.

_[8:13] Scene `now`: void / blood; on screen: typer._

- `8:13` Look again at what the Madina did. A paper that opposed Pakistan, short of newsprint in the middle of a world war, gave months of space to the best case for it, and let readers on both sides answer.
- `8:26` And look at the Amroha letter: a father and son who disagreed about the biggest question of their lives, and kept talking. Whatever we think of the answers, that is how a public argument is supposed to work.
- `8:39` Today, when much of our political argument happens in feeds that show each of us only our own side, a small-town paper in wartime Bijnor sets a high bar. That observation is ours.
