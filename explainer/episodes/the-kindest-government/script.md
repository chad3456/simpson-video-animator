## [0:44] The Kindest Government


## [0:49] Part 1: Why Big Government Feels Kind


_[0:53] Scene `folk`: land / dusk; on screen: arrow, crowd, heart, icon, label._

- `0:53` Saad cites research linking higher empathy to stronger support for government redistribution and welfare programs.
- `1:01` He also leans on the idea of folk economics. Our intuitions evolved in small bands, where food was shared, and one person's gain often was another's loss.
- `1:13` In a modern economy, wealth can grow for everyone, but our instincts still picture a fixed pie.

_[1:19] Scene `harmony`: land / day; on screen: crowd, house, label._

- `1:19` His historical example is New Harmony, Indiana, a utopian community founded by Robert Owen in 1825, where property would be shared.
- `1:31` It collapsed within about two years. Owen's own son concluded that paying the hardworking and the idle the same simply drives the hardworking away.

## [1:41] Part 2: Parasitic Taxation


_[1:45] Scene `taxday`: studio / midnight; on screen: icon, label, meter._

- `1:45` Saad is blunt, and personal, here. He describes the day in 2022 when he paid his bill to the Quebec and Canadian governments, and watched more than half of his best year's income disappear.
- `1:58` Quebec's top combined income tax rate is a little over fifty-three percent.
- `2:03` Add sales tax, property tax and the rest, Saad calculates, and he keeps about a third of what he earns.

_[2:11] Scene `compliance`: studio / teal; on screen: card, heart, icon, label._

- `2:11` He notes that Canada's federal income tax began in 1917, as a temporary wartime measure.
- `2:19` And he cites a lab study where people primed to feel empathy became more willing to pay their taxes. For Saad, that's the mechanism: feelings make the bill go down easier.
- `2:31` Critics answer that taxes buy roads, schools, courts and safety nets, and that high-tax countries like Denmark consistently rank among the happiest and most prosperous on Earth.

## [2:43] Part 3: "Free" Healthcare


_[2:47] Scene `waits`: studio / midnight; on screen: bars, icon, label._

- `2:47` Next, Canada's public healthcare. Saad stresses that it isn't free. It's paid for through taxes.
- `2:54` And waits have grown. A Fraser Institute survey estimated that the median wait from a family doctor's referral to treatment was under ten weeks in 1993, and nearly twenty-eight weeks in 2023.
- `3:09` Many Quebecers wait years for a family doctor, and Saad says he pays for private insurance to get timely care.

_[3:16] Scene `defend-hc`: studio / teal; on screen: card._

- `3:16` Defenders point out that Canada covers everyone, spends far less per person than the United States, and that Canadians don't go bankrupt over hospital bills.

## [3:27] Part 4: The Nanny State


_[3:31] Scene `nanny`: city / dusk; on screen: card, label, pebble._

- `3:31` Saad also mocks what he sees as petty rules: fines for crossing an empty street, a legal date for switching to winter tyres, and Quebec's language office policing the size of French on shop signs.
- `3:44` That's where his chapter title comes from. Faced with all this care, he sarcastically cries out: govern me harder, daddy.
- `3:52` Defenders say many such rules follow real accidents, and that Quebec's language laws protect a minority language on an English-speaking continent.

_[4:02] Scene `military`: land / dusk; on screen: crowd, icon, label._

- `4:02` Finally, he turns to the military, where he argues that recruitment has come to prioritise inclusion over readiness. He cites a reported British Army plan to drop its motto, Be the Best, which in the end it kept.
- `4:17` A military's job, he says, is to deter enemies, and empathy is no substitute for strength.

## [4:24] Part 5: The Case Against


_[4:28] Scene `against`: studio / midnight; on screen: label, scale._

- `4:28` Critics say Saad underrates what government does well. Public pensions sharply cut poverty among the elderly, and vaccination, clean water and safety rules have saved millions of lives.
- `4:41` They also note that markets themselves depend on government: on courts, contracts and property rights.
- `4:48` Shared ground: help should be judged by results, not intentions, and every program should be able to answer a simple question. Is this working, and at what cost?

_[4:59] Scene `outro`: land / dusk; on screen: critter, label, pebble._

- `4:59` Ants are remarkable. But humans aren't ants. We're individuals who cooperate, compete, and want our effort to count.
- `5:07` The challenge, for left and right alike, is building a society that's kind without asking people to stop being human.
- `5:15` That ends our tour through Suicidal Empathy. Agree with Saad or not, his question is worth keeping: is our kindness actually helping?
