## [1:00] Part 1: A School Strike


_[1:04] Scene `schools`: studio / teal; on screen: cite, crowd, hexmap, label, stat._

- `1:04` It started with something unglamorous: a teachers' strike on the seventeenth of September over staff shortages.
- `1:11` Pupils joined in. They described classes of thirty-two to thirty-six, temporary classrooms that hit forty degrees in the summer heat, and an admissions system, Parcoursup, that many dread.
- `1:25` On the twenty-first, a high school in Créteil, south-east of Paris, was blockaded. Within days, dozens of schools in Seine-Saint-Denis and Val-de-Marne followed.
- `1:36` By the first of October, the Interior Ministry counted more than a thousand high schools affected, and nearly two thousand arrests.

_[1:44] Scene `school-violence`: city / midnight; on screen: card, cite, label, pebble, shape._

- `1:44` And it was already violent. By early October, the Education Minister said twenty-four schools had been burned or ransacked, and sixty-five staff injured, forty of them head teachers.
- `1:57` In Marseille, a masked protester turned a flamethrower on a woman who told him to stop the vandalism. Police arrested a fifteen-year-old.
- `2:06` Children were hurt too: a fifteen-year-old lost a hand to a grenade, a sixteen-year-old lost the use of an eye. By mid-October, the police watchdog had opened thirty-six inquiries.

## [2:19] Part 2: Saturday, 17 October


_[2:23] Scene `map-17`: space / midnight; on screen: cite, hexmap, label, stat._

- `2:23` Then, on the seventeenth of October, the school strike merged with a national day of action over fuel prices and the cost of living, joined by unions and the yellow vests.
- `2:35` The Interior Ministry counted five hundred and fifty thousand people in a hundred and ten marches. Unions said eight hundred thousand. Most marched peacefully.
- `2:45` But in city after city, violent groups broke away. In Lyon, a tourist office was torched. In Marseille, a crowd broke into city hall and dozens of shops were looted. In Bordeaux, a masked mob invaded the prefect's residence.
- `3:01` In Saint-Étienne, a seventeen-year-old lost his right hand to a crowd-control grenade. Lille, Perpignan, Strasbourg: the list kept growing.

_[3:12] Scene `paris-17`: city / ember; on screen: car, cite, crowd, label, shape, stat, van._

- `3:12` In Paris, reporters watched masked, black-clad young men smash shop windows with golf clubs and run off with the goods. Banks were smashed, a Lacoste store ransacked, cars set alight.
- `3:24` Nationwide, authorities counted at least three thousand shops damaged or looted.
- `3:30` The Interior Minister said more than five hundred police officers, gendarmes and firefighters were injured. One protester died of a cardiac arrest in Paris.

_[3:41] Scene `aftermath`: studio / midnight; on screen: cite, gbars, icon, label._

- `3:41` By the twentieth, more than twenty-five hundred people had been taken into custody. Over eight hundred went before prosecutors, and a hundred and forty-nine were jailed.
- `3:52` Curfews followed in parts of Paris, Lille and Marseille, and the army was called in to support police for the next weekend.
- `4:00` The Justice Minister admitted the State had, in his word, failed. The Prime Minister admitted intelligence failures. The Interior Minister offered to resign; Macron refused.
- `4:12` There is no official damage figure yet. Early estimates run from half a billion to more than a billion euros, which would rival the riots of 2023.

## [4:23] Part 3: Testing the Claim


_[4:27] Scene `claim`: studio / plum; on screen: cite, icon, label._

- `4:27` Now to the claim that spread fastest: that immigrants burned France, using the students as cover.
- `4:34` It wasn't only anonymous accounts. President Trump told a rally that France was being conquered, looted and burned to the ground by migrants, jihadists and communists, and said that Paris would soon be a Muslim city.
- `4:48` It's a serious claim, so let's test it the way we'd test any other: against arrests, court records and the people actually named.

_[4:57] Scene `evidence`: studio / teal; on screen: card, cite, label._

- `4:57` First, the hard limit. France doesn't record ethnicity or religion, and the authorities have published no nationality data for the people arrested.
- `5:07` What we do have: the Justice Minister says seventy percent of those held were adults, and that very often they were known to police. Early court reports describe a more mixed picture, with many first offenders.
- `5:20` Then there are the people actually named. The man arrested over the Limoges fire is a forty-year-old far-left activist on a security watchlist. A former aide to a hard-left MP was arrested in Saint-Denis.
- `5:35` In Lille, three men in their early twenties with no criminal record got ten months for looting a video game shop. In Perpignan, a thirty-year-old school supervisor was questioned over looting a Sephora.

_[5:49] Scene `officials-experts`: space / midnight; on screen: cite, icon, label._

- `5:49` The French government's own account is different from the viral one. It blames an insurrectionary plan by ultra-left fringe groups, plus opportunistic criminals.
- `6:00` The Associated Press checked the immigrant claims and found that authorities had not linked immigrants to the rioting, and that no single group had been singled out.
- `6:10` The criminologist Sebastian Roché told AP the far left is too small to explain violence on this scale, and that most of the looting looks like ordinary criminals exploiting the chaos.
- `6:23` Macron called claims that France had been invaded by hordes of migrants and Islamists completely false. Note that his own government ordered the 2025 report on Islamist entryism you're about to see.

_[6:38] Scene `banlieue`: city / plum; on screen: crowd, label._

- `6:38` So where does the immigrant claim come from? Partly from geography. The blockades began in Seine-Saint-Denis and Val-de-Marne, the most immigrant-heavy suburbs in France.
- `6:50` That part is real: many of the pupils on those barricades come from immigrant families, and these are the same suburbs where the State itself has documented Islamist networks. We'll come to that.
- `7:03` But no published arrest data, court case or official has yet shown that immigrants as a group drove the arson and looting.
- `7:11` Absence of data is not innocence, either. France refuses to count. Until the courts publish profiles, nobody can prove this either way.

_[7:21] Scene `flags`: city / ember; on screen: cite, crowd, label, text._

- `7:21` And here's something the polite version of this story leaves out. In a protest about class sizes, Palestinian flags kept appearing at the scenes of violence.
- `7:31` Toulouse's statue of Joan of Arc was draped in one. In Paris, a synagogue was broken into, though investigators' first findings point to burglary.
- `7:42` In Marseille, the Jewish community's security service said several damaged shops had Jewish owners, but that nothing yet showed they were targeted for it, and asked people not to share unverified claims.
- `7:55` A flag proves no motive. But Gaza had nothing to do with French classrooms, and some rioters brought it anyway. That's worth saying plainly.

## [8:05] Part 4: Chaos Inc.


_[8:09] Scene `nomani`: space / midnight; on screen: cite, label, planet, stat._

- `8:09` Which brings us to Asra Nomani, a senior investigations editor at Fox News Digital, whose reporting has done the most to map who was behind the protests.
- `8:19` On the ninth of October, she identified fifty organisations backing the movement. Only fifteen were student or youth groups. The rest were adults: unions, communist and socialist groups, four political parties including La France Insoumise, and a Muslim student association.
- `8:38` By the twentieth, her count had grown to a hundred and one groups in thirteen countries, only thirty-three of them led by young people.
- `8:47` She traced amplification to BreakThrough News, an outlet in the network funded by the American millionaire Neville Roy Singham. One of its videos, of the Saint-Denis mayor, drew about three hundred and forty-six thousand likes in a day.
- `9:03` And she documented plans for more: two hundred and twelve rallies for an Acte Two on the twenty-fourth of October, and a European youth strike in November.

_[9:12] Scene `nomani-test`: studio / ember; on screen: card, cite, label._

- `9:12` She frames it as a red-green alliance: far-left reds, and Islamist groups she identifies with green.
- `9:20` What her work does show is that this was never only a student movement. Adult organisations, many with hard-left politics, amplified it at home and abroad. That fits the French government's own intelligence, which called it an operation by La France Insoumise and its far-left proxies.
- `9:39` What it doesn't show is who threw the petrol bombs. Her evidence is mostly the groups' own posts, websites and statements. It doesn't document funding reaching French rioters, and she doesn't claim immigrants did the burning.
- `9:53` Some in the movement didn't help their own case. The Saint-Denis mayor said violence could, at a given moment, be legitimate, and was referred to prosecutors.
- `10:04` La France Insoumise, for its part, calls the network theory conspiracism, and says the protests were spontaneous.

## [10:12] Part 5: The Question Nobody Asks


_[10:16] Scene `growth`: studio / teal; on screen: cite, gbars, label, stat._

- `10:16` The question so many French people ask, and so many explainers dodge: how much has Islam grown in France, and does it matter?
- `10:25` The best official data is the national statistics office's survey of 2019 and 2020. Ten percent of adults aged eighteen to fifty-nine said they were Muslim. Among under-fifties, the share rose from eight to eleven percent in about a decade.
- `10:43` And it is the most durable faith in the country. Ninety-one percent of people raised Muslim stay Muslim, the highest rate of any religion measured.
- `10:53` At the same time, most of France is losing religion altogether. So Islam is growing in a country that is emptying its churches. That's a real shift, and it's fair to talk about it.

_[11:05] Scene `entryism`: space / midnight; on screen: card, cite, label._

- `11:05` Then there's what the French State itself found. In May 2025, a report commissioned by the government on the Muslim Brotherhood was put before a defence council.
- `11:17` It counted a hundred and thirty-nine places of worship linked to the Brotherhood and sixty-eight close to it, about two hundred and eighty associations, and twenty-one schools teaching some forty-two hundred pupils.
- `11:31` By then, the State had closed seven hundred and forty-one establishments after more than nine and a half thousand inspections.
- `11:39` The presidency's own phrase for the strategy was entryism from below: not bombs, but slow influence through mosques, schools, sport and charities. That isn't a conspiracy theory. It's a government finding.

_[11:53] Scene `ifop`: studio / plum; on screen: cite, gbars, label, stat._

- `11:53` Polling points the same way, though it's more contested. A 2025 Ifop survey of about a thousand French Muslims found that forty-five percent of young Muslim women wear a veil, nearly three times the share in 2003.
- `12:09` Forty-six percent said Islamic law should be applied where they live, fully or in part.
- `12:16` And a third said they felt sympathy for at least one Islamist movement.
- `12:21` Four Muslim councils filed a legal complaint, calling the poll biased, and some academics agree. But the trend it shows, younger and more observant, matches what the State's own report describes.

_[12:34] Scene `link`: space / plum; on screen: cite, label, scale._

- `12:34` So, does this connect to the riots? Here's where we have to be precise rather than polite, in either direction.
- `12:41` Nomani's list of groups backing the movement included a Muslim student association, alongside La France Insoumise and communist groups. That's her red-green alliance, and the overlap is real.
- `12:54` What nobody has shown yet is that Islamist networks planned or lit the fires of the seventeenth. The only person charged over the worst single fire is a French far-left activist. Two problems can be true at once.

## [13:10] Part 6: The France That Wasn't


_[13:13] Scene `fakes`: studio / midnight; on screen: cite, feed._

- `13:13` Online, a second France was burning, one that never existed.
- `13:18` An AI video of a Palestinian flag on the Eiffel Tower got three point seven million views. Another, of the tower on fire, got eleven point six million. A clip blaming African men for the protests was from 2017.
- `13:34` A claim that looters only targeted Jewish shops was rejected by police and by the Jewish community's own security service. And the Marseille flamethrower attack, which was real, was sold as an anti-white hate crime by a migrant. Police have confirmed neither.
- `13:52` France even summoned Iran's ambassador over what it called a state disinformation campaign. Disinformation was coming from more than one direction.

## [14:03] Part 7: Suicidal Empathy


_[14:07] Scene `voices`: studio / plum; on screen: cite, label, quote._

- `14:07` Which brings us back to Gad Saad's idea of suicidal empathy: compassion aimed so badly that it protects the people doing harm and abandons the people being harmed.
- `14:18` In February 2025, Rima Hassan, a French member of the European Parliament for La France Insoumise, told Sud Radio that Hamas has a legitimate action. In the same interview she called its methods terrorist.
- `14:34` On the twentieth of October, three days after the riots, Lola-Fleur Whittaker, a Sciences Po student who ran on a France Insoumise list in Paris, told LCI the violence was a response to decades of misery.
- `14:49` When a shop owner pushed back, she was reported to have answered: your little shops, that's real cute.

_[14:55] Scene `victims`: city / midnight; on screen: cite, house, label, pebble, quote._

- `14:55` Now look at who paid. In Lyon, a florist filmed her smashed shop: the till ripped out, flowers trampled, her seventy-year-old mother inside. They wanted to hit us, she said. We just want to work in peace.
- `15:09` The shop owner on that television set was a woman who came to France from the Congo at nine. She said the shop fed her and around a hundred other people.
- `15:19` In Grenoble, a florist whose shop was hit wrote to the Prime Minister to say she won't pay her taxes. The empathy was spent on the rioters. The bill went to women like these.
- `15:31` That's the pattern Saad warns about, and the October riots fit it: sympathy flowing to whoever claims to be oppressed, while the actual victims, French-born and immigrant alike, are told their shops don't matter.

## [15:44] Part 8: What We Know


_[15:48] Scene `verdict`: studio / teal; on screen: card, label._

- `15:48` So, as of today, the scorecard. Supported: violent ultra-left groups and criminals used a real student protest as cover, adult political networks amplified it, and the French State has itself documented Islamist entryism in the same country.
- `16:05` Not supported by any evidence published so far: that immigrants as a group burned France, or that shops were picked by religion.
- `16:14` Still unknown: the full profile of the people arrested, and who organised the worst of the arson. France should publish that data. Refusing to count only feeds the rumours.

_[16:26] Scene `lens`: space / plum; on screen: cite, heart, label._

- `16:26` Saad's warning cuts deepest here. A mayor calling violence legitimate, a politician calling Hamas's action legitimate, a student shrugging at ruined shops: that is empathy aimed at the wrong target.
- `16:40` The cure isn't the opposite mistake. It's counting honestly, naming who actually did what, and refusing to look away, whoever that turns out to be.

_[16:50] Scene `outro`: space / midnight; on screen: label, pebble, planet._

- `16:50` Next time a city burns, three questions are worth asking before anything else.
- `16:55` Who was actually arrested, and for what? Who is excusing it, and why? And is this video even real?
