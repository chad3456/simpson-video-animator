- `0:00` In 1847, a doctor in Vienna noticed that women in his maternity ward were dying far more often when the doctors came straight from performing autopsies.
- `0:12` Ignaz Semmelweis told his colleagues to wash their hands. Deaths plunged. And much of the medical establishment scoffed at him.
- `0:21` He died in an asylum. Germ theory later proved him right.

_[0:25] Scene `open-marshall`: studio / midnight; on screen: book, icon, label._

- `0:25` In 1984, the Australian doctor Barry Marshall was so sure that bacteria, not stress, caused most stomach ulcers that he drank a culture of them to prove it. He later shared a Nobel Prize.
- `0:39` In chapter five of Suicidal Empathy, Gad Saad tells stories like these to make a point. In science, the consensus can be wrong, and the word settled should make us nervous.

## [0:57] Part 1: How Science Works


_[1:01] Scene `popper`: studio / teal; on screen: arrow, card, label._

- `1:01` The philosopher Karl Popper argued that a scientific claim has to be falsifiable: there must be some result that could prove it wrong.
- `1:10` That makes science humble by design. Every theory is the best one so far, never the final word.
- `1:17` Saad's worry is that the phrase settled science is now used less as a summary of evidence, and more as an emotional appeal: if you question this, you must not care about people.

_[1:29] Scene `tradeoffs`: studio / midnight; on screen: label, quote, scale._

- `1:29` The psychologist Philip Tetlock describes taboo trade-offs: we refuse to weigh sacred values, like health or safety, against things like money or freedom, because even doing the sums feels monstrous.
- `1:43` But every policy trades something for something else.
- `1:46` As the economist Thomas Sowell put it, there are no solutions, only trade-offs.

## [1:51] Part 2: The Pandemic


_[1:55] Scene `ifr`: studio / midnight; on screen: bars, label, text._

- `1:55` COVID is Saad's main example. Before vaccines, researchers estimated that the infection fatality rate for people under sixty was about three or four in ten thousand. For older people, it was many times higher.
- `2:11` That steep age gradient, he argues, should have shaped policy: protect the vulnerable, and weigh the costs of closing schools and businesses for everyone else.

_[2:21] Scene `curfew`: city / midnight; on screen: card, crowd, icon, label._

- `2:21` Instead, Quebec imposed Canada's first curfew in January 2021, and in many places schools stayed closed for months.
- `2:30` And in June 2020, more than a thousand public health professionals signed a letter supporting mass anti-racism protests, while other gatherings were still banned. Saad sees that as moral feelings deciding what counted as a health risk.

_[2:47] Scene `covid-other`: studio / plum; on screen: label, scale._

- `2:47` Critics answer that early on, no one knew the numbers. Hospitals in places like Lombardy and New York were overwhelmed, and vaccines are estimated to have saved millions of lives in their first year.
- `3:00` Both things can be true. Emergency measures saved lives, and the costs of some of them deserved more open debate than they got.

## [3:09] Part 3: Climate and Children


_[3:13] Scene `climate`: land / day; on screen: icon, label, pebble, planet._

- `3:13` Saad applies the same lens to climate change.
- `3:16` He isn't denying warming. His target is the emotional climate around it: groups like BirthStrike, whose members pledged not to have children because of the climate crisis.
- `3:28` To an evolutionary psychologist, choosing not to reproduce out of empathy for the planet is about as pure an example of suicidal empathy as there is.
- `3:39` Climate scientists broadly agree that humans are warming the planet. The real debate is about trade-offs: how fast to cut emissions, at what cost, and who pays.

## [3:50] Part 4: Gender Medicine


_[3:54] Scene `cass`: studio / plum; on screen: book, icon, label, text._

- `3:54` Saad's most heated example is gender medicine for young people.
- `3:59` In 2024, England's Cass Review, a four-year independent study, concluded that the evidence for puberty blockers in children was remarkably weak.
- `4:10` That same year, the New York Times reported that the American researcher Johanna Olson-Kennedy had not published results from a large federally funded study of puberty blockers, saying she worried they could be weaponized. The results reportedly showed no improvement in mental health.

_[4:29] Scene `sport`: studio / midnight; on screen: card, icon, label._

- `4:29` He also points to sport, where a 2024 UN report counted hundreds of medals lost by female athletes to male-born competitors, and to a California prison case, where an inmate who transferred to a women's prison was later accused of sexually assaulting women there.
- `4:48` On the other side, several major US medical bodies still support individual care, and trans people face high rates of distress and discrimination that deserve real compassion.
- `5:01` Where both sides should agree: young patients deserve the best evidence, published in full, whatever it shows.

## [5:08] Part 5: The Case Against


_[5:12] Scene `against`: studio / midnight; on screen: card, label._

- `5:12` Saad's critics say he picks the cases where the consensus wobbled, and skips the many where it held: vaccines, smoking and cancer, evolution itself.
- `5:23` Questioning science is healthy. Treating every expert as a fraud isn't. Most of the time, the consensus is the best bet we have.
- `5:32` Shared ground: science earns trust by showing its work, admitting mistakes, and letting people disagree out loud.

_[5:40] Scene `outro`: studio / teal; on screen: crowd, icon, label._

- `5:40` Semmelweis wasn't right because he was a rebel. He was right because he had data, and the establishment wouldn't look at it.
- `5:47` The lesson cuts both ways. Follow the evidence, especially when it's inconvenient, and especially when someone tells you that asking questions is unkind.
