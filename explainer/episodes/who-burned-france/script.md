## [0:58] Part 1: A School Strike


_[1:01] Scene `schools`: studio / teal; on screen: cite, crowd, hexmap, label, stat._

- `1:01` It started with something unglamorous: a teachers' strike on the seventeenth of September over staff shortages.
- `1:09` Pupils joined in. They described classes of thirty-two to thirty-six, temporary classrooms that hit forty degrees in the summer heat, and an admissions system, Parcoursup, that many dread.
- `1:22` On the twenty-first, a high school in Créteil, south-east of Paris, was blockaded. Within days, dozens of schools in Seine-Saint-Denis and Val-de-Marne followed.
- `1:33` By the first of October, the Interior Ministry counted more than a thousand high schools affected, and nearly two thousand arrests.

_[1:42] Scene `school-violence`: city / midnight; on screen: card, cite, label, pebble, shape._

- `1:42` And it was already violent. By early October, the Education Minister said twenty-four schools had been burned or ransacked, and sixty-five staff injured, forty of them head teachers.
- `1:54` In Marseille, a masked protester turned a flamethrower on a woman who told him to stop the vandalism. Police arrested a fifteen-year-old.
- `2:04` Children were hurt too: a fifteen-year-old lost a hand to a grenade, a sixteen-year-old lost the use of an eye. By mid-October, the police watchdog had opened thirty-six inquiries.

## [2:17] Part 2: Saturday, 17 October


_[2:20] Scene `map-17`: space / midnight; on screen: cite, hexmap, label, stat._

- `2:20` Then, on the seventeenth of October, the school strike merged with a national day of action over fuel prices and the cost of living, joined by unions and the yellow vests.
- `2:32` The Interior Ministry counted five hundred and fifty thousand people in a hundred and ten marches. Unions said eight hundred thousand. Most marched peacefully.
- `2:43` But in city after city, violent groups broke away. In Lyon, a tourist office was torched. In Marseille, a crowd broke into city hall and dozens of shops were looted. In Bordeaux, a masked mob invaded the prefect's residence.
- `2:59` In Saint-Étienne, a seventeen-year-old lost his right hand to a crowd-control grenade. Lille, Perpignan, Strasbourg: the list kept growing.

_[3:09] Scene `paris-17`: city / ember; on screen: car, cite, crowd, label, shape, stat, van._

- `3:09` In Paris, reporters watched masked, black-clad young men smash shop windows with golf clubs and run off with the goods. Banks were smashed, a Lacoste store ransacked, cars set alight.
- `3:22` Nationwide, authorities counted at least three thousand shops damaged or looted.
- `3:27` The Interior Minister said more than five hundred police officers, gendarmes and firefighters were injured. One protester died of a cardiac arrest in Paris.

_[3:39] Scene `aftermath`: studio / midnight; on screen: cite, gbars, icon, label._

- `3:39` By the twentieth, more than twenty-five hundred people had been taken into custody. Over eight hundred went before prosecutors, and a hundred and forty-nine were jailed.
- `3:49` Curfews followed in parts of Paris, Lille and Marseille, and the army was called in to support police for the next weekend.
- `3:57` The Justice Minister admitted the State had, in his word, failed. The Prime Minister admitted intelligence failures. The Interior Minister offered to resign; Macron refused.
- `4:09` There is no official damage figure yet. Early estimates run from half a billion to more than a billion euros, which would rival the riots of 2023.

## [4:20] Part 3: Testing the Claim


_[4:24] Scene `claim`: studio / plum; on screen: cite, icon, label._

- `4:24` Now to the claim that spread fastest: that immigrants burned France, using the students as cover.
- `4:31` It wasn't only anonymous accounts. President Trump told a rally that France was being conquered, looted and burned to the ground by migrants, jihadists and communists, and said that Paris would soon be a Muslim city.
- `4:45` It's a serious claim, so let's test it the way we'd test any other: against arrests, court records and the people actually named.

_[4:54] Scene `evidence`: studio / teal; on screen: card, cite, label._

- `4:54` First, the hard limit. France doesn't record ethnicity or religion, and the authorities have published no nationality data for the people arrested.
- `5:04` What we do have: the Justice Minister says seventy percent of those held were adults, and that very often they were known to police. Early court reports describe a more mixed picture, with many first offenders.
- `5:18` Then there are the people actually named. The man arrested over the Limoges fire is a forty-year-old far-left activist on a security watchlist. A former aide to a hard-left MP was arrested in Saint-Denis.
- `5:32` In Lille, three men in their early twenties with no criminal record got ten months for looting a video game shop. In Perpignan, a thirty-year-old school supervisor was questioned over looting a Sephora.

_[5:46] Scene `officials-experts`: space / midnight; on screen: cite, icon, label._

- `5:46` The French government's own account is different from the viral one. It blames an insurrectionary plan by ultra-left fringe groups, plus opportunistic criminals.
- `5:57` The Associated Press checked the immigrant claims and found that authorities had not linked immigrants to the rioting, and that no single group had been singled out.
- `6:07` The criminologist Sebastian Roché told AP the far left is too small to explain violence on this scale, and that most of the looting looks like ordinary criminals exploiting the chaos.
- `6:21` And Macron, asked about foreign leaders, said claims that France had been invaded by hordes of migrants and Islamists were completely false, and the comments as unacceptable as they are stupid.

_[6:34] Scene `banlieue`: city / plum; on screen: crowd, label._

- `6:34` So where does the immigrant claim come from? Partly from geography. The blockades began in Seine-Saint-Denis and Val-de-Marne, the most immigrant-heavy suburbs in France.
- `6:45` That part is real: many of the pupils on those barricades come from immigrant families. But a teenager protesting a class of thirty-six is not evidence of an invasion.
- `6:57` What hasn't been shown, by any published arrest data, court case or official, is that immigrants as a group drove the arson and looting, or that they used the students as cover.
- `7:09` Could the picture change? Yes. When courts publish fuller profiles, we'll know more. But today, the claim runs ahead of the evidence.

## [7:17] Part 4: Chaos Inc.


_[7:21] Scene `nomani`: space / midnight; on screen: cite, label, planet, stat._

- `7:21` Which brings us to Asra Nomani, a senior investigations editor at Fox News Digital, whose reporting has done the most to map who was behind the protests.
- `7:32` On the ninth of October, she identified fifty organisations backing the movement. Only fifteen were student or youth groups. The rest were adults: unions, communist and socialist groups, four political parties including La France Insoumise, and a Muslim student association.
- `7:51` By the twentieth, her count had grown to a hundred and one groups in thirteen countries, only thirty-three of them led by young people.
- `7:59` She traced amplification to BreakThrough News, an outlet in the network funded by the American millionaire Neville Roy Singham. One of its videos, of the Saint-Denis mayor, drew about three hundred and forty-six thousand likes in a day.
- `8:15` And she documented plans for more: two hundred and twelve rallies for an Acte Two on the twenty-fourth of October, and a European youth strike in November.

_[8:25] Scene `nomani-test`: studio / ember; on screen: card, cite, label._

- `8:25` She frames it as a red-green alliance: far-left reds, and Islamist groups she identifies with green.
- `8:32` What her work does show is that this was never only a student movement. Adult organisations, many with hard-left politics, amplified it at home and abroad. That fits the French government's own intelligence, which called it an operation by La France Insoumise and its far-left proxies.
- `8:51` What it doesn't show is who threw the petrol bombs. Her evidence is mostly the groups' own posts, websites and statements. It doesn't document funding reaching French rioters, and she doesn't claim immigrants did the burning.
- `9:06` Some in the movement didn't help their own case. The Saint-Denis mayor said violence could, at a given moment, be legitimate, and was referred to prosecutors.
- `9:16` La France Insoumise, for its part, calls the network theory conspiracism, and says the protests were spontaneous.

## [9:25] Part 5: The France That Wasn't


_[9:28] Scene `fakes`: studio / midnight; on screen: cite, feed._

- `9:28` Online, a second France was burning, one that never existed.
- `9:33` An AI video of a Palestinian flag on the Eiffel Tower got three point seven million views. Another, of the tower on fire, got eleven point six million. A clip blaming African men for the protests was from 2017.
- `9:49` A claim that looters only targeted Jewish shops was rejected by police and by the Jewish community's own security service. And the Marseille flamethrower attack, which was real, was sold as an anti-white hate crime by a migrant. Police have confirmed neither.
- `10:07` France even summoned Iran's ambassador over what it called a state disinformation campaign. Disinformation was coming from more than one direction.

## [10:18] Part 6: What We Know


_[10:21] Scene `verdict`: studio / teal; on screen: card, label._

- `10:21` So, as of today, here's the scorecard. Supported: violent fringes and opportunists used a real protest as cover, and adult political networks amplified it far beyond the schools.
- `10:35` Not supported by any evidence published so far: that immigrants as a group burned France, or that shops were picked by religion.
- `10:43` Still unknown: the full profile of the people arrested, and who, if anyone, organised the worst of the arson. Those answers will come from the courts, not from viral videos.
