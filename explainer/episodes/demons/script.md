# LUMEN Documentary: "Demons: The Revolution That Ate Its Own"

*A paper-collage video essay on Dostoevsky's Demons: the real 1869 murder behind it, the liberal fathers and nihilist sons, Shigalyov's paradise for one tenth, the fashionable people who flattered the radicals, three kinds of freedom, and the strongest case against Dostoevsky's verdict on the revolutionary left.*

## [1:29] A LUMEN documentary: DEMONS (The revolution that ate its own)


## [1:35] Part one: A murder
in the park (Moscow, 1869. Siberia, 1849.)


_[1:40] Scene `p1-nechayev`: paperc / cream; on screen: cite, cut, headline, hl, scrib._

- `1:40` Start with the real story. Sergei Nechayev was twenty-two, a young schoolteacher from a poor family in the textile town of Ivanovo.
- `1:49` In 1869 he arrived in Moscow claiming to speak for a vast secret committee that would soon raise all of Russia. There was no committee.
- `2:00` He organised students into small cells of five. Each member knew only his own cell, and obeyed orders that came, supposedly, from above.
- `2:10` His little organisation called itself the People's Reprisal.

_[2:15] Scene `p1-catechism`: paperc / news; on screen: cite, hl, scrib, sheet._

- `2:15` Around the same time, a short text began to circulate: the Catechism of a Revolutionary. Historians still argue over whether Nechayev wrote it alone, or with the anarchist Mikhail Bakunin.
- `2:28` It opens: the revolutionary is a doomed man. He has no personal interests, no feelings, no attachments, not even a name.
- `2:37` And it states the rule plainly. Everything that helps the revolution is moral. Everything that stands in its way is immoral and criminal.
- `2:46` Hold on to that sentence. The whole novel is a long, hard look at what happens to people who believe it.

_[2:53] Scene `p1-ivanov`: paperc / cream; on screen: cite, cut, hl, park, stampc._

- `2:53` One cell member, a student named Ivan Ivanov, began asking questions. He doubted the committee existed, and he refused an order.
- `3:03` Nechayev told the others that Ivanov was about to betray them.
- `3:07` On the twenty-first of November, they lured him to a grotto in the park of the Petrovsky Agricultural Academy. Nechayev strangled him and then shot him, while the others held him down.
- `3:20` The killing was meant to remove a doubter. It was also meant to bind the survivors to their leader with shared guilt.
- `3:27` The body was found within days. Nechayev fled to Switzerland. Dozens of young people were arrested, and in 1871 their trial became a public sensation.

_[3:40] Scene `p1-dresden`: paperc / news; on screen: cite, cut, headline, sheet._

- `3:40` Dostoevsky was living in Dresden, broke, homesick and gambling, and he followed the case in the Russian papers.
- `3:47` He had a personal link. His wife Anna's brother was a student at that very academy, and had visited them with stories about the mood among the young.
- `3:57` Dostoevsky was already planning a novel. Now it had its spine.

_[4:01] Scene `p1-1849`: paperc / cream; on screen: cite, headline, hl, stakes, stampc._

- `4:01` But here is what makes Demons more than an attack. Dostoevsky had once been one of them.
- `4:07` In the 1840s he belonged to a circle of young socialists in St Petersburg. In 1849 he was arrested, partly for reading a forbidden letter aloud.
- `4:21` On a December morning he was led onto a city square. Prisoners in white shrouds were tied to posts in threes, and the soldiers raised their rifles.
- `4:31` At the last moment, a pardon from the Tsar was read out. The whole execution had been staged.
- `4:37` He spent four years in a Siberian prison camp, among murderers and thieves, with the New Testament as his only book.

_[4:45] Scene `p1-insider`: paperc / cream; on screen: cite, cut, hl._

- `4:45` Years later he wrote that he could never have been a Nechayev. But a Nechayevist, a follower? He would not swear to it. Perhaps, in his youth, he could have been.
- `4:55` So Demons is not a book about monsters. It is about decent young people, and how an idea can lead them, one reasonable step at a time, to a grotto beside a frozen pond.

## [5:08] Part two: The fathers (Where the sons got their ideas)


_[5:12] Scene `p2-stepan`: paperc / sage; on screen: cite, cut, hl._

- `5:12` Dostoevsky does not begin with the revolutionaries. He begins with their parents.
- `5:18` Meet Stepan Trofimovich Verkhovensky, a handsome, ageing liberal of the 1840s. He once gave a few brilliant university lectures, and has been living on the memory ever since.
- `5:32` He believes he is a persecuted man, under watch by the government. Mostly, nobody is watching.
- `5:39` He lives on the estate of a rich widow, Varvara Petrovna, who pays his debts, chooses his clothes, and loves him in a way neither of them can admit.

_[5:50] Scene `p2-bill`: paperc / cream; on screen: cite, cut, hl, scrib, sheet._

- `5:50` Stepan speaks beautifully about humanity, beauty and progress, usually in French, often with tears in his eyes, generally with a glass of champagne. He is lovable, and Dostoevsky clearly likes him.
- `6:03` And then the novel quietly shows you the bill. Years ago, Stepan lost a card game and paid the debt with a human being: his serf Fedka, handed over to the army as a recruit.
- `6:15` Fedka deserted, and became a convict and a killer. He is in town now, and he will be back.
- `6:22` Stepan also had a son, Pyotr. He sent the boy away as a baby to be raised by relatives, barely saw him for twenty years, and lived off the income from the boy's small estate.
- `6:34` He loved humanity. The specific humans in his care, rather less.

_[6:38] Scene `p2-thesis`: paperc / news; on screen: cite, cut, headline, hl, scrib._

- `6:38` That is Dostoevsky's first, and most provocative, thesis. The nihilist sons are not a rebellion against their liberal fathers. They are the fathers' ideas, with the manners removed.
- `6:51` In a letter to the heir to the throne, he wrote that the gentle idealists of the 1840s would never believe it, but they were the direct fathers of the Nechayevs.
- `7:02` The fathers cut morality loose from God and tradition, and put eloquence in its place. The sons kept the conclusion, and threw away the eloquence.
- `7:12` It is a contested claim, and we will come back to it. But it gives the novel its shape: a comedy about the fathers that turns, chapter by chapter, into a tragedy about the sons.

## [7:24] Part three: The fixer (How to build a cell out of shame)


_[7:29] Scene `p3-pyotr`: paperc / mustard; on screen: cite, cut, hl, stampc._

- `7:29` Enter Pyotr Stepanovich, the abandoned son, now in his late twenties and back from abroad.
- `7:35` He talks fast, fawns, lies, flatters and is always busy. He is charming in the way that makes you check your wallet.
- `7:43` Pyotr is Dostoevsky's Nechayev. He tells the local radicals that he represents a central committee and a vast network of cells. Like Nechayev, he is bluffing.

_[7:55] Scene `p3-cell`: paperc / cream; on screen: cell, cite, hl._

- `7:55` His cell of five is not a band of supermen. It is a provincial gossip, a mild-mannered clerk, a gloomy theorist, a post-office clerk who plays the piano, and a self-appointed expert on the common people.
- `8:09` None of them is a monster. They are bored, vain, a little lonely, and terrified of looking backward.

_[8:16] Scene `p3-meeting`: paperc / news; on screen: cite, crowdp, headline, sheet._

- `8:16` Their big gathering takes place at Virginsky's name-day party, disguised as a celebration.
- `8:22` The progressives spend the first half hour voting on whether they are, in fact, having a meeting. Someone proposes a vote on the vote.
- `8:31` A schoolboy lectures his elders. A young woman declares that marriage is slavery. Everyone is competing to be the most radical person in the room.

_[8:41] Scene `p3-test`: paperc / cream; on screen: cite, crowdp, cut, hl, scrib._

- `8:41` Then Pyotr springs a test. Suppose, he asks, each of you knew that a political murder was being planned. Would you go to the police?
- `8:50` Nobody wants to be the one who says yes. To say yes would be to look like a coward, or worse, a reactionary.
- `8:57` Only Shatov, a former member who has broken with the movement, walks out in disgust. And with that, Pyotr has found his victim, and his glue.

_[9:07] Scene `p3-glue`: paperc / cream; on screen: cite, cut, headline, hl._

- `9:07` Stavrogin, half in mockery, has already handed Pyotr the method. Persuade four members of a circle to kill a fifth, and the blood they share will bind them for good. Pyotr loves the idea.
- `9:20` He has a second tool, even simpler. Russians, he says, can be won over most easily by offering them the right to be dishonourable. Offer that, and they will all come running.
- `9:31` This is the novel's sharpest observation about moral movements. Pyotr does not recruit through belief. He recruits through shame, the fear of not being progressive enough.
- `9:43` Watch how often, in Demons, decent people go along with something terrible, because objecting would make them look old-fashioned.

## [9:52] Part four: One tenth (Shigalyov's paradise)


_[9:56] Scene `p4-shig`: paperc / cream; on screen: cite, cut, scrib, sheet._

- `9:56` Every movement needs a theorist. Pyotr's is Shigalyov, a gloomy man with enormous ears and a thick notebook, who has worked out the entire future organisation of society.
- `10:08` He presents it at the meeting, and he is honest enough to admit there is a problem.

_[10:14] Scene `p4-quote`: paperc / night; on screen: cite, cut, hl._

- `10:14` SHIGALYOV: I am perplexed by my own data. Starting from unlimited freedom, I arrive at unlimited despotism.
- `10:20` That is the line everyone remembers, and it is not a joke about hypocrisy. Shigalyov is sincere. He followed the logic all the way down, and that is where it led.

_[10:32] Scene `p4-tenth`: paperc / cream; on screen: cite, headline, herd, hl._

- `10:32` His solution is to divide humanity into two unequal parts. One tenth receives absolute freedom, and unlimited power over the other nine tenths.
- `10:43` The nine tenths lose their individuality and become something like a herd. After generations of re-education, they reach a kind of primeval innocence: an earthly paradise.
- `10:55` Paradise, with ninety per cent of the people as livestock. Some of the listeners are appalled. Others think it sounds rather practical.

_[11:04] Scene `p4-shigalyovism`: paperc / mustard; on screen: cite, cut, hl._

- `11:04` Later, alone with Stavrogin, Pyotr gives his own version of the theory, and drops the philosophy.
- `11:11` PYOTR: Cicero will have his tongue cut out, Copernicus will have his eyes put out, Shakespeare will be stoned. That's Shigalyovism. Slaves are bound to be equal.
- `11:19` Talent is suspect, because talent creates inequality. Everyone spies on everyone else and reports. Equality is reached by bringing everyone down.

_[11:30] Scene `p4-destroy`: paperc / oxblood; on screen: cite, headline, hl._

- `11:30` PYOTR: We shall proclaim destruction. We'll set fires going. We'll set legends going. There's going to be an upheaval such as the world has never seen.
- `11:38` That is the real goal, and it slips out in his own words. Not a plan for justice. A plan for upheaval.

_[11:45] Scene `p4-pretender`: paperc / slate; on screen: cite, cut, hl._

- `11:45` After the chaos, Pyotr needs a face. He wants Stavrogin, handsome, aristocratic and mysterious, presented to the people as a hidden prince, Ivan the Tsarevich.
- `11:57` The revolution for equality ends with a pretender on a throne. Pyotr even admits he is not a socialist at all. He is a scoundrel, he says, laughing.
- `12:08` That is Dostoevsky's charge in its sharpest form. Strip away the language of equality and justice, and some revolutionaries want one thing: power, and the thrill of tearing things down.

_[12:21] Scene `p4-two`: paperc / cream; on screen: cut, hl, scrib._

- `12:21` But notice that Shigalyov and Pyotr are different people. One is a sincere idealist whose logic leads to slavery. The other is a cynic who knows how to use idealists.
- `12:33` Dostoevsky thinks you need to recognise both, because a movement needs both, and each makes the other possible.

## [12:40] Part five: Rebellion
in fashion (Those who flatter the radicals)


_[12:45] Scene `p5-salon`: paperc / cream; on screen: cite, cut, hl, strings._

- `12:45` The radicals are not the only target. Dostoevsky saves some of his sharpest satire for the respectable people who flatter them.
- `12:53` Yulia Mikhailovna, the governor's wife, wants to be the patron of the young. She thinks she can steer the radicals by being nice to them, and Pyotr runs her like a puppet.
- `13:05` Her husband, Governor von Lembke, is a decent, anxious bureaucrat who writes a novel in secret, and slowly loses his grip.
- `13:14` And there is Karmazinov, the famous writer. A celebrated liberal, he grovels before the young nihilists so as not to seem out of date, while quietly preparing to leave Russia for good.
- `13:26` Readers recognised Ivan Turgenev, Dostoevsky's great rival, in this portrait. It was cruel, and Turgenev was hurt.

_[13:34] Scene `p5-fete`: paperc / news; on screen: cite, crowdp, cut, headline, hl._

- `13:34` It all comes together at Yulia's grand fête: a literary matinée followed by a ball, for the benefit of poor governesses.
- `13:43` Karmazinov reads a very long farewell to literature, called Merci. The audience is bored stiff.
- `13:50` Then Stepan Trofimovich takes the stage and, to his great credit, says the unfashionable thing.
- `13:57` STEPAN: Shakespeare and Raphael are higher than the emancipation of the serfs, higher than socialism, higher than the young generation, higher almost than humanity itself!
- `14:07` The radicals of the 1860s liked to say a pair of boots was worth more than Shakespeare. Stepan is booed off the stage, and scandal follows scandal.

_[14:19] Scene `p5-boots`: paperc / cream; on screen: balance, hl._

- `14:19` It sounds like a joke. But it is the oldest question in the book: is a person, or a poem, worth anything that cannot be measured by its use to the cause?
- `14:30` Stepan, the vain old liberal, gets this one right. And the crowd hates him for it.

_[14:35] Scene `p5-fire`: paperc / night; on screen: cite, cut, headline, stampc, town._

- `14:35` That night, across the river, the district of Zarechye catches fire.
- `14:40` In one of its houses, Captain Lebyadkin and his sister Marya, the lame, simple woman secretly married to Stavrogin, are found murdered.
- `14:49` The killer is Fedka, the convict Stepan once lost at cards, paid with money that leads back to Pyotr.
- `14:56` The governor goes to the fire and loses his mind. The crowd turns into a mob. Lisa, a young woman who loved Stavrogin, is beaten to death in the street.
- `15:07` Nobody in this town wanted any of it. Almost everyone helped.

## [15:12] Part six: Three kinds
of freedom (Kirillov, Shatov, Stavrogin)


_[15:16] Scene `p6-three`: paperc / cream; on screen: cut, hl, strings._

- `15:16` Beneath the satire, Demons is a serious argument about freedom. Dostoevsky makes it through three of the most remarkable characters he ever wrote.
- `15:26` Each was once a disciple of Stavrogin. Each took one of his ideas, and lived it out to the end.

_[15:33] Scene `p6-kirillov`: paperc / sage; on screen: cite, cut, headline, hl._

- `15:33` Kirillov is an engineer: quiet, kind, a little strange. He plays ball with his landlady's child, and loves the leaves on the trees.
- `15:42` His idea is this. People cling to life because they fear pain, and fear what comes after. God, for him, is the name of that fear.
- `15:52` Anyone who kills himself for no reason except to prove that he is free will abolish the fear, and become a god himself.

_[16:00] Scene `p6-kirillov2`: paperc / night; on screen: cite, hl, sheet._

- `16:00` KIRILLOV: If there is no God, then I am God.
- `16:03` It is rebellion pushed to the absolute: freedom understood as self-will, with nothing above it. And it ends, logically, with a pistol.
- `16:12` Pyotr, being Pyotr, has made a deal with him. Since Kirillov plans to die anyway, he will first write a note taking the blame for whatever the cell needs.

_[16:23] Scene `p6-shatov`: paperc / cream; on screen: cite, cut, hl, scrib._

- `16:23` Shatov is Kirillov's opposite. The son of a serf, clumsy and passionate, he has broken with the radicals and now preaches that Russia has a special, God-bearing mission.
- `16:34` Stavrogin asks him the one question he cannot dodge. But do you yourself believe in God?
- `16:40` SHATOV: I believe in Russia. I believe in her Orthodoxy. I believe in the body of Christ. I will believe in God.
- `16:48` I will believe. Not I do. Dostoevsky gives something close to his own convictions to a man who is not sure of them. That honesty is part of why the book still works.

_[16:59] Scene `p6-stavrogin`: paperc / slate; on screen: cite, cut, hl, sheet._

- `16:59` And at the centre is Nikolai Stavrogin: handsome, strong, rich, fearless, and completely empty.
- `17:06` In his youth, out of boredom, he pulled a respectable old man by the nose, bit the governor's ear, and secretly married a disabled woman, as a kind of joke on himself.
- `17:18` Everyone projects their dreams onto him. Pyotr sees a prince, Shatov a prophet, Kirillov a teacher. Stavrogin believes nothing at all.
- `17:27` He is what absolute freedom looks like when it has nowhere to go. He can do anything, and so nothing matters.

_[17:35] Scene `p6-tikhon`: paperc / cream; on screen: cite, cut, hl, sheet._

- `17:35` In a chapter called At Tikhon's, Stavrogin visits a retired bishop and hands him a printed confession. It includes a crime against a young girl, which we will not describe.
- `17:47` The magazine's editor, Mikhail Katkov, refused to print it. The novel appeared without it, and the chapter was not published until 1922.
- `17:57` Tikhon reads it and sees straight through it. Even the confession is a performance. Stavrogin wants to shock people, not to repent.

_[18:06] Scene `p6-letter`: paperc / night; on screen: cite, hl, sheet._

- `18:06` Near the end, Stavrogin writes a letter that may be the most chilling thing in the book.
- `18:12` STAVROGIN: I can still wish to do something good, and feel pleasure in it. At the same time I wish to do evil, and feel pleasure in that too.
- `18:19` His rage has no object, and his rebellion has no content. That, Dostoevsky suggests, is exactly the void that men like Pyotr rush in to fill.

## [18:30] Part seven: The night
in the park (Shatov, Kirillov, and the end of the cell)


_[18:35] Scene `p7-marie`: paperc / cream; on screen: candle, cite, cut, hl._

- `18:35` And now the novel returns to that grotto by the pond.
- `18:39` On the night before the murder, Shatov's wife Marie, who left him years ago, turns up unexpectedly, and goes into labour. The child is Stavrogin's.
- `18:49` Shatov does not care. He runs for the midwife, borrows tea and a samovar from Kirillov, and is happier than he has ever been in his life.
- `18:58` SHATOV: There were two of us, and now suddenly there is a third human being: a new spirit, whole and complete.

_[19:05] Scene `p7-park`: paperc / night; on screen: cite, headline, hl, park._

- `19:05` The next evening the cell lures Shatov to Stavrogin's park, on the pretext that he must hand over a hidden printing press.
- `19:13` Pyotr shoots him in the forehead. They weigh the body down with stones and drop it into the pond.
- `19:19` Virginsky keeps crying out that this is not right. Lyamshin screams. Erkel, the youngest and most idealistic, calmly does exactly as he is told.

_[19:30] Scene `p7-kirillov`: paperc / night; on screen: cite, emptyroom, sheet, stampc._

- `19:30` Then Pyotr goes to collect his alibi. Kirillov, horrified to learn that Shatov is dead, writes the note anyway, claiming the murder as his own.
- `19:40` And then he shoots himself. Dostoevsky stages it as a nightmare, one of the most frightening scenes in literature. We will leave it there.

_[19:49] Scene `p7-collapse`: paperc / cream; on screen: cell, cite, headline, train._

- `19:49` The group falls apart within days. Lyamshin breaks down and confesses everything to the police, and the others are arrested.
- `19:57` And Pyotr? He takes the train to St Petersburg, and slips abroad. The organiser is the only one who walks away.

_[20:05] Scene `p7-uri`: paperc / slate; on screen: cite, hl, sheet._

- `20:05` Stavrogin goes home to his estate, climbs up to the attic, and hangs himself with a silk cord that he has carefully soaped.
- `20:13` His last letter mentioned that he had become a citizen of a quiet Swiss canton, Uri, and might go and live there. He never went.

## [20:22] Part eight: Into the swine (Stepan's last journey)


_[20:27] Scene `p8-road`: paperc / sage; on screen: cite, cut, hl._

- `20:27` And Stepan Trofimovich? The old liberal who started it all walks out of his comfortable life in the rain, with a bag and an umbrella, to go and find the real Russia.
- `20:38` He gets about as far as the next village, falls ill, and meets a woman who sells copies of the Gospels from door to door, Sofya Matveyevna.

_[20:48] Scene `p8-reading`: paperc / night; on screen: candle, cite, openbook._

- `20:48` He asks her to read to him, and she reads the passage about the demons and the swine.
- `20:53` And Stepan finally understands. The demons that went out of the sick man, he says, are all the sores, all the poisons, that have gathered in our great, sick Russia over the centuries.
- `21:05` STEPAN: That's us. Us, and them, and my Petrusha, and I, perhaps, at the head of them all.
- `21:11` It is the only moment in the novel when someone accuses himself first.

_[21:16] Scene `p8-swine`: paperc / sage; on screen: cite, hl, pigs._

- `21:16` The demons rush into the swine, and the swine run off the cliff. But that is not the end of the passage.
- `21:23` The healed man sits at Jesus's feet, clothed and in his right mind. Dostoevsky wrote to a friend that this was his hope for Russia: sick, possessed, and one day healed.
- `21:35` Stepan dies a few days later. His last words are about love, and about the need to bow down before something infinitely greater than yourself.

## [21:44] Part nine: Prophecy, and
the case against (Was Dostoevsky right?)


_[21:49] Scene `p9-history`: paperc / cream; on screen: cite, ptl._

- `21:49` Within a decade, Russian revolutionaries really were using terror. In 1881 a group called the People's Will assassinated Tsar Alexander the Second.
- `22:01` In 1917 the Bolsheviks took power: a disciplined party of professional revolutionaries, with a secret police, and a doctrine in which the cause decided what was moral.
- `22:14` Within twenty years, old revolutionaries were standing up at show trials, confessing to crimes they had not committed, as the revolution devoured its own.

_[22:24] Scene `p9-readers`: paperc / news; on screen: cite, cut, hl._

- `22:24` Readers saw Shigalyov everywhere. The philosopher Nikolai Berdyaev, writing in 1918, called Dostoevsky the prophet of the Russian revolution.
- `22:35` Albert Camus, who adapted Demons for the stage in 1959, counted it among the handful of books he valued above all others.
- `22:45` On the other side, in 1913 the writer Maxim Gorky protested that a Moscow stage version was harmful to society. Under Soviet rule the novel was treated for decades as reactionary slander.

_[23:00] Scene `p9-lenin`: paperc / cream; on screen: hl, stampc._

- `23:00` A memoir by Lenin's associate Vladimir Bonch-Bruevich claims that Lenin admired Nechayev's talent as an organiser. Historians treat that single source with some caution.
- `23:12` But the echo is hard to miss: the party of the few, the cause above every rule, and the comrade who doubts becoming the enemy.

_[23:20] Scene `p9-against`: paperc / news; on screen: cite, headline, sheet._

- `23:20` So, was Dostoevsky right? Here is the strongest case against him.
- `23:25` First, the caricature. Most Russian radicals of his day were not Nechayevs. Many were idealists who went to the villages to teach and to heal, and paid for it with prison and exile.
- `23:37` Even inside the movement, Nechayev was condemned. Bakunin broke with him in 1870 over his lies and his blackmail, and a new circle, the Chaikovtsy, formed partly to prove that radicals could be honest.
- `23:53` Second, the family tree. Plenty of liberals of the 1840s raised sons who became doctors and reformers, not murderers. The line from Stepan to Pyotr is a novelist's argument, not a historian's proof.
- `24:09` Third, Dostoevsky's own politics. He published Demons in a conservative journal, revered the monarchy, and in his later journalism wrote in a nationalist and at times antisemitic way. His cure, an Orthodox Russian revival, was a political program too.
- `24:27` And the book is a satire. It is unfair on purpose, as good satire usually is.

_[24:33] Scene `p9-yet`: paperc / cream; on screen: cite, cut, hl, scrib._

- `24:33` And yet. What makes Demons last is not that Dostoevsky hated the left. It is that he understood a movement from the inside, because he had once been inside one.
- `24:44` His argument is not that wanting justice is wrong. It is that a cause which decides for itself what is moral can justify anything, and that the loudest moralists are often the least examined.
