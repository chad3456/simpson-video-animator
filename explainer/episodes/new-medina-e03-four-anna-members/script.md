# LUMEN E3: "Four-Anna Members"

*Shut out of power in 1937, the Muslim League took on Nehru's campaign to win Muslims with economics. Episode 3 follows the by-elections, the war over flags, songs and schools, a Sufi scholar's telegram and a bald parrot from Rumi, as a party 'neither dead nor alive' became a mass movement.*

- `0:33` The wire that went out from his lodge said only this: do not vote for the Congress.

## [1:05] Chapter One: Mass Contact


_[1:10] Scene `nehru`: void / ink; on screen: cite, cquote, dossier, typer._

- `1:10` Congress had swept the province in 1937. But every one of its nine candidates for Muslim seats in the United Provinces had lost.
- `1:20` Nehru was undaunted. Muslims, he said, had simply not been asked. He would go over the heads of their 'reactionary' leaders, straight to the masses, with whom, he said, the economic reality was bound to prevail.
- `1:34` The communal problem, as Nehru saw it, was a quarrel among the middle classes over jobs and seats. The peasant cared about hunger. Give him bread, land and freedom, and religion would take care of itself.

_[1:47] Scene `ashraf`: room / sepia; on screen: cite, cquote, dossier, typer._

- `1:47` To run the campaign Nehru picked K. M. Ashraf, a communist, and set him up at Anand Bhavan, the Nehru family home in Allahabad.
- `1:56` Ashraf had been a pious young man. As a student he began a forty-day Sufi ordeal of fasting and prayer. On the thirteenth night he dreamt he was drinking cheap wine with a Hindu friend. His teacher told him that spiritual progress was not his destiny. Years later, in London, he found Marx.
- `2:14` He did not believe Muslims were a political unit at all. Politics, he wrote, is essentially dictated by class interests. A Muslim peasant and a Muslim landlord were enemies, whatever they prayed.

_[2:27] Scene `hindustan`: void / ink; on screen: cite, paper, typer._

- `2:27` The campaign launched an Urdu weekly, Hindustan. Its writers explained Muslim backwardness in the language of Marx: feudal lords, a missing bourgeoisie, the betrayal of Sir Syed.
- `2:39` Its practical aim was to sign Muslims up as four-anna members of Congress, a quarter of a rupee a year.
- `2:46` Ashraf even denied there was any such thing as a single Muslim culture. What passed for it, he wrote, was the culture of a tiny feudal elite, and the masses had nothing to do with it. It was a bold argument. It would prove to be a gift to his opponents.

## [3:02] Chapter Two: Massacre Contact


_[3:07] Scene `iqbal`: room / archive; on screen: cite, cquote, doc, typer._

- `3:07` The League saw the threat at once. The poet-philosopher Muhammad Iqbal wrote to Jinnah: the world must be told that the economic problem is not the only problem in the country. For Muslims, the cultural problem mattered more.
- `3:21` Jinnah went further. He called the Congress campaign 'massacre contact', and told Muslims not to be led away by the cries of dal-bhat, rice and lentils. Bread was being offered in exchange for identity, he implied, and Muslims should refuse the trade.

_[3:37] Scene `twoanna`: void / ink; on screen: cite, typer._

- `3:37` Then the League copied its rival, and undercut it. Congress charged four annas a year. The League charged two.
- `3:44` It rewrote its constitution with elected branches from the tehsil up, and adopted a new goal: a free and independent India with a democratic form of government. On paper it now sounded as radical as Congress.
- `3:59` Its U.P. branch set itself a target of enrolling a quarter of the province's adult Muslims within three months.

_[4:07] Scene `mahmudabad`: void / jade; on screen: cite, cquote, dossier, typer._

- `4:07` The League's answer to socialism was not to ignore it. It was to claim it. Its chief organiser in the province was the young Raja of Mahmudabad: a great landlord who wore homespun khaddar, a pious Shia, and one of the few men close to Jinnah.
- `4:22` Islam, the Raja told audiences, was the oldest socialist creed in the world. The Prophet had been an orphan and knew poverty. The League's programme promised minimum wages, fair rents and an end to usury.
- `4:36` One supporter put the League's case in a single line: when the slogan is 'Workers of the World Unite', nobody has a problem. When it is 'Muslims of the World Unite', everybody has a problem!

_[4:48] Scene `lucknow37`: void / ink; on screen: cite, cquote, samap, typer._

- `4:48` In October 1937 the League met in Lucknow, and the leaders of the Muslim-majority provinces came: Sir Sikandar Hayat Khan of Punjab and Fazlul Haq of Bengal.
- `5:00` The Governor reported that Jinnah had given Muslims a very strong and definite communal lead, and that it had inspired great enthusiasm. Sikandar told his own Governor that a Congressman at that session would have been lynched.
- `5:14` Jinnah, the immaculate barrister in Savile Row suits, now appeared in public in a sherwani and a fur cap.

## [5:22] Chapter Three: A War of Symbols


_[5:27] Scene `wardha`: void / ink; on screen: cite, doc._

- `5:27` Out of the legislatures, the League found its sharpest weapon in the symbols of the new Congress governments. First came Gandhi's Wardha scheme: seven years of village schooling built around a craft like spinning, taught in the mother tongue, without religious instruction. Its syllabus was drafted by a committee under a Muslim educationist, Dr Zakir Husain.
- `5:48` A League committee under the Raja of Pirpur tore it apart. It called the scheme Gandhian totalitarianism, likened its teaching of non-violence to the race doctrines of fascist states, and rejected the idea that all religions teach the same truths. Muslims, it said, needed their own education boards, run by Muslims.

_[6:08] Scene `symbols`: void / smoke; on screen: card, cite, typer._

- `6:08` Then came language, song and flag. The League attacked Hindustani as a disguise for Sanskritised Hindi, and the singing of Bande Mataram and the flying of the tricolour in schools as Hindu impositions.
- `6:22` Congress was incredulous. The song spoke of thirty crore Indians, all of them. The flag stood for every community. Only months earlier Khaliquzzaman himself had invited a Congress leader to raise that tricolour over Lucknow's municipal building.
- `6:38` But the League had found its theme. In 1938 it unfurled a flag of its own, adopted an anthem of its own, and at Patna it gave Jinnah a title to set beside the Mahatma's: Qaid-i-Azam, the Great Leader.

_[6:53] Scene `symverdict`: void / ink; on screen: cite, verdict._

- `6:53` Dhulipala's point is that this war of symbols did more than score points. By refusing the nation's song and flag, the League was laying the ground for a claim that Muslims were a nation of their own. On that, most historians agree.
- `7:08` The League's charge of Congress tyranny is a different matter. Its reports alleged atrocities. The Governor did not believe Congress had abetted them. But the historian Mukul Kesavan has shown that well-known local Congressmen did organise the legal defence of Hindus arrested in riots. The truth sits uncomfortably in between.

## [7:29] Chapter Four: Six By-Elections


_[7:33] Scene `jhansi`: void / ink; on screen: cite, doc, panel, samap, typer._

- `7:33` The test came at the ballot box. In Jhansi the Congress candidate, Nisar Sherwani, was an outsider and broke. His brother wrote to Nehru from a sickbed about the heartless neglect of the so-called socialist party in power. The party's star Muslim orators would not come.
- `7:51` Nehru took charge himself. He raised seven hundred rupees on his own surety, told the candidate that bicycles were good enough and motor cars a waste, and arranged loudspeakers and workers for all eighty polling booths.
- `8:05` The League ran on a single cry: Islam in danger. Shaukat Ali, the old Khilafat hero, spoke of a coming civil war and of making a 'Spain of India'. When Nehru came to campaign, League supporters stoned his car.

_[8:19] Scene `letter`: room / archive; on screen: cite, doc._

- `8:19` Then fate took a hand. A letter from Nehru to the Congress manager, Rafi Ahmad Kidwai, was delivered by mistake to the League candidate, Rafiuddin Ahmad.
- `8:29` Pro-League papers printed a version of it which, they claimed, showed Congress paying the clerics who campaigned for it. Congress later published the real text to refute the charge. It made no difference. Those clerics were branded as men who had sold their community for money, a charge that followed them for the rest of British rule.

_[8:48] Scene `tally`: void / ink; on screen: cite, panel, samap, typer._

- `8:48` Bijnor, in October, went the other way. The League told voters that a vote for Congress was a vote for unbelief, and accused the Congress candidate of apostasy. He won anyway, with seventy percent. One League orator who claimed the pro-Congress clerics had been bribed was taken to court by the local paper, Madina, and had to apologise.
- `9:09` But in December the League took Moradabad, Saharanpur and Bulandshahr, all rural seats where poorer Muslims had been expected to back Congress. In 1938 it took Badayun with three quarters of the vote. Of the contested by-elections, Congress won one. The League won five.

_[9:27] Scene `bier`: void / smoke; on screen: cite, figures, typer._

- `9:27` League posters in Moradabad showed the Congress Muslim candidate carried on a Hindu bier to the cremation ground, with the caption: contrast the beginning to the end. Join Congress, it meant, and you would die an unbeliever.
- `9:41` Congress blamed landlords who herded their tenants to the polls, and money. Some of that was surely true. But it could not explain why the gap kept widening.

## [9:51] Chapter Five: The Hakim al-Ummat


_[9:55] Scene `thanawi`: void / ink; on screen: cite, cquote, dossier, typer._

- `9:55` The man behind that telegram, Ashraf Ali Thanawi, was perhaps the most influential Islamic scholar in South Asia. His guide to women's conduct, Bihishti Zewar, was part of almost every bride's dowry in north India. He despised Gandhi. But at first he thought little better of the League. It was, he told his circle, a one-eyed man. Congress was a blind one.
- `10:17` He sent long questionnaires to both sides. The Congress-allied Jamiat ignored his. The League's provincial president answered every question in detail.
- `10:27` Muslims inside Congress, the League argued, would always be outvoted: one Muslim on a Working Committee of twenty-one. Only standing apart, as a nation of their own, would make the world listen.

_[10:40] Scene `patna`: room / archive; on screen: cite, figures, slate, typer, verdict._

- `10:40` Thanawi's fatwa backing the League was read out at its session in Patna by his disciple Zafar Ahmad Usmani. Usmani later left an account of what happened there. The clerics met Jinnah the night before, he said, and told him that Muslims were a religious community. Jinnah answered that religion and politics should be kept apart. That, the clerics replied, was a European idea.
- `11:02` The next day, Usmani recalled, Jinnah told the session that Islam was not just a religion but a total way of life.
- `11:10` At one o'clock the session stopped for prayers. Jinnah, worried about whether a Sunni or a Shia should lead, stood behind the town's qazi, and the whole gathering stood behind him. It is a memorable scene, but it comes from one partisan memoir, written years later.

_[11:26] Scene `parrot`: void / smoke; on screen: cite, dossier, samap, typer._

- `11:26` Thanawi's real target was a fellow Deobandi, Husain Ahmad Madani. Madani taught that Hindus and Muslims of India were one nation, and found a precedent in the covenant the Prophet made with the Jews of Medina. Thanawi answered that in that covenant Muslims led and the Jews followed, and the Prophet judged their disputes. No such terms bound Congress.
- `11:49` Then he mocked his rival with a story from Rumi. A grocer strikes his parrot and leaves it bald. Later a bald monk walks past, and the parrot cries out: did you spill the oil too? The bird took a stranger for its equal. So, Thanawi implied, did clerics who thought themselves fit to reinterpret the tradition like the great scholars of the past. When Deoband welcomed a Hindu Congress leader at its station, Thanawi resigned as its patron, and had his resignation posted on the seminary's gates.

## [12:21] Chapter Six: Deliverance


_[12:25] Scene `members`: void / ink; on screen: cite, cquote, dots, typer._

- `12:25` By early 1938, Congress said it had enrolled a hundred thousand Muslims. The League claimed three hundred thousand. Both were party figures, but the direction was clear.
- `12:37` Riots tore through the province. By the middle of 1938 the mass contact programme had quietly been shut down. Ashraf wrote to Nehru that he was left doing routine office work, and felt like a parasite.

_[12:51] Scene `deliver`: room / archive; on screen: cite, cquote, doc._

- `12:51` Then, in November 1939, the Congress ministries resigned in protest at India being taken into the war without its consent. Jinnah called on Muslims to celebrate a Day of Deliverance, with prayers of thanks after Friday worship.
- `13:07` The Raja of Mahmudabad told Jinnah it showed, without ambiguity, that there were not one but several nations in India.
- `13:15` Others saw less. Some League men refused to take part. Nehru called it a failure, and the new Governor reported that meetings were smaller than expected. The League had won the argument against Congress. Now it had to say what it was for. Three months later, at Lahore, it tried.

## [13:32] Chapter Seven: What the Historians Say


_[13:36] Scene `tests`: void / smoke; on screen: verdict._

- `13:36` So what holds up? That the League out-mobilised Congress among U.P. Muslims between 1937 and 1939: well supported, by the by-election results. The membership numbers are claims, so set them aside.
- `13:51` That the Deobandi scholars around Thanawi were a critical and neglected factor: supported, with caveats. The fatwas and posters are on record. How many votes they moved, no one can say.
- `14:04` That mass contact failed because it spoke only of bread and ignored faith: debated. Congress's own letters show neglect, money troubles and quarrels, and the riots did their own damage.
- `14:16` And the old claim that Congress's refusal to share power in 1937 created Pakistan: contested. It was made by men on both sides. Dhulipala's point is that what the League built after that refusal mattered more.

_[14:32] Scene `now`: void / blood; on screen: typer._

- `14:32` Strip away the names and this chapter describes an argument that has never ended. One side says people vote their interests: wages, land, prices. The other says people vote who they are.
- `14:44` In 1937 Nehru bet on bread. The League bet on belonging, and won. Politicians from India to Europe to America are still placing the same bet today. That last thought is ours, not the book's.
