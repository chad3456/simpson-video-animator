# LUMEN E1: "The Séance"

*Seven years after his death, a seance summons the founder of Pakistan, and he sees 'flashes of evil pictures'. Episode 1 of a ten-part documentary on Venkat Dhulipala's Creating a New Medina: the claim that Pakistan was not a vague accident but a plentifully imagined Islamic state, a new Medina, argued over in the towns of north India, and the critics who disagree.*

Source: Venkat Dhulipala, Creating a New Medina: State Power, Islam, and the Quest for Pakistan in Late Colonial North India (Cambridge University Press, 2015); reviews by F. Devji (The Wire, 2015), S. I. Khan (Marginalia Review), P. B. Mehta (Indian Express, 2015), I. Talbot (JAS, 2015); A. Jalal, The Sole Spokesman (1985).

_[0:21] Scene `seance`: room / archive; on screen: cite, seance, slate, typer._

- `0:21` Six in the evening, the thirteenth of March, 1955. Pakistan is eight years old. Its founder, Mohammad Ali Jinnah, has been dead for nearly seven.
- `0:33` A government officer, a certain Mr Ibrahim, has hired a spiritualist to call him back.
- `0:39` The spiritualist politely offers the guest a seat. The spirit answers, rather tartly, that it is already seated.
- `0:47` In life, the Qaid-i-Azam was a heavy smoker. So a cigarette is lit and fixed on a wire stand, for the spirit to smoke while it answers.
- `0:56` *(music beat, 2.4s)*

_[0:59] Scene `transcript`: void / ink; on screen: cite, cquote, doc._

- `0:59` Mr Ibrahim asks the father of the nation to guide its destiny. The spirit refuses. That is no longer its place, it says, though it often sees 'flashes of evil pictures about Pakistan'.
- `1:12` Is there a prosperous future? 'I don't think so,' comes the reply. Its only advice to Pakistan's rulers is selflessness.
- `1:20` And then a line that historians keep coming back to.
- `1:23` 'It is easier to acquire a country,' says the spirit, 'but it is extremely difficult to retain it.'
- `1:30` *(music beat, 2.0s)*

_[1:32] Scene `crisis`: void / blood; on screen: cite, rule, typer, year._

- `1:32` Whatever one makes of talking to the dead, the document records something real: a sense of crisis, almost from the start.
- `1:40` Jinnah died in September 1948, barely a year after independence. There was war with India over Kashmir. In 1951 the prime minister, Liaquat Ali Khan, was shot dead.
- `1:54` Secularists, clerics, Islamists and regional parties could not agree on a constitution. Governments came and went. In 1958 came the first martial law.
- `2:05` Coups, insurgencies, sectarian killing, and leaders assassinated or driven into exile have followed ever since.

_[2:13] Scene `question`: void / ink; on screen: tome, typer._

- `2:13` Why was the new country in trouble so soon? For decades, one answer has dominated the history books.
- `2:21` Pakistan, it says, was never really imagined. It was a slogan, a bargaining chip, a country that came about almost by accident.
- `2:29` This series follows a historian who went into the archives and came back with the opposite answer.
- `2:36` His name is Venkat Dhulipala. His book is called Creating a New Medina.

## [2:41] A LUMEN DOCUMENTARY IN TEN PARTS: Creating a New Medina (Episode 1 · The Séance)


## [2:49] Chapter One: The Accidental Country


_[2:54] Scene `partition`: void / ink; on screen: cite, samap, typer._

- `2:54` In August 1947 the British left, and their Indian empire was cut in two.
- `3:01` India. And Pakistan, in two wings a thousand miles apart.
- `3:05` The new borders ran through Punjab and Bengal. Millions crossed them in both directions. Historians estimate that between two hundred thousand and two million people were killed.
- `3:17` How did something so vast happen so fast? Here the historians divide.

_[3:22] Scene `jalal`: void / ink; on screen: cite, cquote, scale, tome, typer._

- `3:22` In 1985 Ayesha Jalal published The Sole Spokesman, and changed the field.
- `3:28` Jinnah, she argued, did not really want a separate sovereign state. Pakistan was a bargaining counter, a demand meant to win Muslims an equal share of power in a loose, undivided India.
- `3:41` When Congress turned down the 1946 Cabinet Mission plan, with its weak centre, the bargain fell apart. Partition was what was left.
- `3:51` And the Muslims who cheered for Pakistan? In her account, it remained 'little more than a catch-all, an undefined slogan'.

_[3:59] Scene `consensus`: void / ink; on screen: cite, tome, typer._

- `3:59` Others disagreed with Jalal about Jinnah. Anita Inder Singh argued he wanted a sovereign Pakistan all along. But even she agreed that for ordinary Muslims, it 'meant all things to all Muslims'.
- `4:12` Gyanendra Pandey, a historian of ordinary lives, wrote that few Muslims had clear ideas of what the goal meant. David Gilmartin saw Pakistan as a symbol of solidarity more than a place on a map.
- `4:26` Christophe Jaffrelot called it a nationalism without a nation. Farzana Shaikh blamed Pakistan's later troubles on that missing content. Salman Rushdie's phrase stuck: insufficiently imagined.
- `4:40` At rallies, crowds chanted: what is the meaning of Pakistan? There is no god but God. Beyond that, the consensus says, there was little content at all.

_[4:49] Scene `standard`: void / smoke; on screen: typer, verdict._

- `4:49` So the standard story runs like this. A vague dream below, a high-stakes bargain above, and a country nobody quite planned.
- `4:58` But there is one place that story struggles to explain.

## [5:02] Chapter Two: The Province Left Behind


_[5:06] Scene `upmap`: void / ink; on screen: cite, doc, samap, typer._

- `5:06` The United Provinces of Agra and Oudh. Today, Uttar Pradesh. The old heartland of Muslim north India: Lucknow, Aligarh, Deoband.
- `5:16` Muslims here were a minority, about fourteen percent of the province. Yet this is where the idea of Pakistan found its earliest, loudest and most sustained support.
- `5:27` In March 1940, at Lahore, the Muslim League demanded that the Muslim-majority areas of the north-west and the east become 'autonomous and sovereign'.
- `5:38` Look at the map. The United Provinces were not in it. Whatever Pakistan became, these Muslims would stay behind, in Hindustan.

_[5:47] Scene `paradox`: void / blood; on screen: cite, stat, typer._

- `5:47` So why would they fight for a country they would never live in?
- `5:51` In the winter of 1945 and 1946, elections became a referendum on Pakistan. In the United Provinces the League swept every Muslim seat for the central assembly, and won about two thirds of the Muslim vote for the province.
- `6:08` An American intelligence report still called that a relatively poor showing, and blamed religious orthodoxy, Sunni-Shia splits and League infighting. The bar was that high.

## [6:19] Chapter Three: A Symbol With Substance


_[6:24] Scene `argue`: paper / ink; on screen: cite, doc, paper._

- `6:24` Dhulipala starts from a simple observation. India was, in Amartya Sen's word, argumentative. Pakistan did not escape the argument.
- `6:34` From the moment the Lahore Resolution passed, it was argued over in Urdu newspapers, in pamphlets and books, at meetings in cities, bazaars and small market towns across north India.
- `6:46` Through those arguments, he says, Pakistan stopped being vague. It gained a shape, a substance and a following.

_[6:53] Scene `medina`: void / jade; on screen: cite, samap, typer._

- `6:53` The shape it took in the United Provinces was this: a sovereign Islamic state. Some of its champions had a name for it. A new Medina.
- `7:02` Medina was the city where the Prophet Muhammad built the first Muslim community and state, in the seventh century. To invoke it was to promise a fresh beginning.
- `7:13` Pakistan would be a refuge for India's Muslims. But more than a refuge: the base for a renewal of Islam in the modern world.
- `7:21` And the heir to the Ottoman Caliphate, abolished in 1924, as the leading power and protector of the world's Muslims.

_[7:30] Scene `geobody`: void / ink; on screen: cite, icon, samap, typer._

- `7:30` League propagandists sold the country's body as well as its soul. They printed its maps, listed its rivers, ports, railways and resources, and praised its place beside the Muslim Middle East.
- `7:43` Bigger than most European states, they said, and potentially a first-class power, stronger than Turkey. One senior leader, Chaudhry Khaliquzzaman, imagined it gathering the Muslim nations into an 'Islamistan'.
- `7:57` In Cairo in 1946, Jinnah told an American diplomat that without Pakistan the Middle East itself would be threatened by what he called Hindu imperialism.
- `8:08` Pakistan would even be a laboratory, where Islam and modern learning would find answers to the problems of the modern world.

_[8:16] Scene `hostages`: void / blood; on screen: cite, scale, typer, verdict._

- `8:16` And the Muslims left behind? Here the propaganda turned cold. It was called the hostage population theory.
- `8:24` Hindus and Sikhs inside Pakistan would be hostages. If Hindu India mistreated its Muslims, Pakistan could answer in kind. Each minority would guarantee the other's safety.
- `8:36` Some went further. In 1940 the Sindh League leader Abdullah Haroon compared India's Muslims with the Sudeten Germans, and referred admiringly to Hitler's move to 'liberate' them.
- `8:49` Jinnah asked why, if Gladstone's Britain could intervene in Armenia to protect a minority, Pakistan should not do the same for its minorities in Hindustan.

_[9:00] Scene `mosque`: void / ink; on screen: cite, doc, verdict._

- `9:00` Was it all a bargaining chip, then? Jinnah himself warned his followers in 1941 that it would be 'a great mistake' to believe Congress claims that Pakistan was only a counter for bargaining.
- `9:12` On an Islamic state, he kept his public words vague. A visitor from the Jamaat-i-Islami later recalled Jinnah's answer: first secure the land for the mosque; then decide how to build the mosque.

_[9:25] Scene `constitution`: room / archive; on screen: cite, doc._

- `9:25` Soon after Lahore, the United Provinces League set up a committee of politicians and religious scholars to draft an Islamic constitution for Pakistan. For reasons never fully explained, its report stayed unpublished until 1957.
- `9:42` Its chairman, Sulaiman Nadwi, was invited in 1949 to lead the experts advising Pakistan's Constituent Assembly. His recommendations drew on that report.

## [9:54] Chapter Four: Two Medinas


_[9:59] Scene `madani`: void / ink; on screen: cite, dossier, typer._

- `9:59` Now the twist at the heart of the book. The first man to hold up Medina as a model for modern India was an opponent of Pakistan.
- `10:08` Maulana Husain Ahmad Madani of Deoband had taught the Prophet's traditions in Medina itself for over a decade. He led the Jamiatul Ulama-i-Hind, the main body of India's religious scholars.
- `10:22` In Medina, he argued, the Prophet had made a covenant with the city's Jews: one people of many faiths. Hindus and Muslims could likewise be one nation. He called it Muttahida Qaumiyat, composite nationalism.
- `10:37` His critics pounced. The scholar Ashraf Ali Thanawi, the poet Muhammad Iqbal and the Islamist Abul Ala Mawdudi all attacked his reading. Muslims, they insisted, were a nation apart.

_[10:50] Scene `usmani`: void / jade; on screen: cite, dossier, samap, typer._

- `10:50` Then Thanawi's disciple, Maulana Shabbir Ahmad Usmani, turned Medina around. Pakistan, he declared, would be the new Medina.
- `10:59` As the Prophet's Medina had been the base from which Islam spread across Arabia and beyond, Pakistan would unite the world's Muslims and carry them back onto the world stage as a great power.
- `11:11` In 1945 Usmani founded the Jamiatul Ulama-i-Islam and campaigned for the League. India's religious scholars split down the middle.
- `11:22` One city, two futures. A Medina of many faiths sharing one homeland. Or a Medina as the capital of a rising Islamic power.
- `11:31` *(music beat, 1.6s)*

_[11:33] Scene `ulamav`: void / smoke; on screen: verdict._

- `11:33` That some leading clerics backed Pakistan is well documented. That they bridged the gap between League politicians and ordinary believers, and so helped win the vote, is Dhulipala's argument.
- `11:46` Other historians weigh it differently. We will put it to the test in episode eight.

## [11:52] Chapter Five: The Great Argument


_[11:57] Scene `ambedkar`: void / ink; on screen: cite, dossier, tome._

- `11:57` Who set the terms of the debate? Dhulipala's surprising answer is a lawyer from Bombay who belonged neither to the League nor to Congress: B. R. Ambedkar.
- `12:07` His book, Thoughts on Pakistan, weighed the case for and against with cold precision. When Gandhi and Jinnah met in Bombay in 1944, both cited it as the authority.
- `12:20` Two constitutional lawyers from Bombay, as the book calls them. Jinnah, who wanted Pakistan. And Ambedkar, who showed India how to argue about it.

_[12:30] Scene `questions`: void / ink; on screen: card, cite._

- `12:30` Put Ambedkar together with the religious scholars who opposed Pakistan, and you get the questions that filled the newspapers for six years.
- `12:39` Would Pakistan be an Islamic state, or a Western-style democracy? Could it pay for its government and army? Where exactly would its borders run?
- `12:49` Could it defend itself against a far larger India? What of its Hindu and Sikh minorities, and the Muslims left behind? Would populations be exchanged? What would it count for in the world?
- `13:01` Questions like these, Dhulipala argues, are what turned a slogan into a programme.

_[13:07] Scene `gandhi`: void / ink; on screen: cite, cquote, doc._

- `13:07` Even Gandhi sharpened it. In July 1942 he wrote in his paper Harijan that Pakistan was nothing like a new linguistic province. It meant carving out of India a wholly independent, sovereign state.
- `13:21` Jinnah replied that Gandhi had put the Muslim demand 'in a nutshell'.
- `13:26` In 1944 the two men argued over maps. Gandhi held to a formula drawn up by C. Rajagopalachari. Jinnah dismissed it as offering a 'mutilated, truncated, and moth-eaten' Pakistan.
- `13:40` Whatever else Pakistan was by then, it was not vague. The battle lines were being drawn on paper.

_[13:47] Scene `cinema`: room / smoke; on screen: cite, figures, particles, samap, shape, slash, slate, typer._

- `13:47` Bombay, the fourteenth of April, 1946. The four o'clock show of a film called Forty Crores: a plea for the unity of India's four hundred million people.
- `13:58` On screen, Hindu and Muslim heroes gather around a map of India and vow to defend its unity.
- `14:05` In the audience, League supporters set off firecrackers and stand up shouting. One of them runs to the screen and slashes it across with a six-inch blade.
- `14:15` *(music beat, 2.6s)*

## [14:18] Chapter Six: The Case Against


_[14:23] Scene `critics1`: void / ember; on screen: cite, cquote, typer._

- `14:23` A book that overturns a consensus collects critics, and this one did. Here are their strongest points.
- `14:29` Faisal Devji, an Oxford historian, wrote that the book was so intent on refuting Ayesha Jalal that it produced 'a travesty of her argument'. Jalal, he said, treated partition as one option among several, not as impossible.
- `14:44` He also argued that reading the movement mainly as religion flattens the League's modernist politics, shaped at Aligarh, and ignores its wider international setting.
- `14:55` Sohaib Khan, writing in the Marginalia Review, praised the 'density of its archival knowledge', but found its idea of what counts as religious too loose, and its readings of sources too seamless.

_[15:08] Scene `critics2`: void / ember; on screen: cite, typer._

- `15:08` Above all, Khan objected to the leap from the 1940s to Pakistan's present. Tying today's crises to the founding vision, he argued, risks making the future look settled at birth.
- `15:21` Pratap Bhanu Mehta, in the Indian Express, called it among the most important studies of Pakistan's ideological origins, while noting striking absences: Maulana Azad, Congress's leading Muslim, and Iqbal's great work of religious thought.
- `15:37` Ian Talbot, David Gilmartin and Gail Minault welcomed it as a major intervention. And Dhulipala names his own limit: his evidence comes from a minority province. Punjab and Bengal, where Pakistan was actually built, need the same study.

_[15:54] Scene `scorecard`: void / smoke; on screen: verdict._

- `15:54` So where does the evidence stand? In this series, every big claim gets a label.
- `15:59` That Pakistan was argued over loudly and in detail in the 1940s, in print and in public meetings: well supported. The newspapers survive.
- `16:10` That many League leaders and leading clerics sold it as an Islamic state, a new Medina: supported, with the caveat that Jinnah kept his public words ambiguous.
- `16:20` That ordinary voters shared that vision, and that Jinnah never meant it as a bargaining chip: debated by historians.
- `16:28` And that Pakistan's later troubles grow from this founding vision: contested. It is the book's boldest claim, and the series will keep testing it.

## [16:37] Chapter Seven: Why It Matters Now


_[16:42] Scene `now-pk`: void / ink; on screen: cite, samap, typer._

- `16:42` Why tell this story now?
- `16:44` Because the argument never ended. In 1949 Pakistan's Constituent Assembly declared that sovereignty belongs to God. In 1971 the eastern wing broke away as Bangladesh. The fight over what Pakistan is for has never stopped.
- `17:02` In 2018 a prime minister, Imran Khan, came to power promising Riyasat-e-Madina: a state modelled on Medina. The idea from the 1940s had come back, almost word for word.

_[17:16] Scene `now-in`: void / smoke; on screen: cite, tome, typer._

- `17:16` In India, partition is not history either. Since 2021 the government has marked the fourteenth of August as Partition Horrors Remembrance Day, and the two-nation theory is still invoked in arguments over citizenship and belonging.
- `17:32` The book was drawn into those arguments too. Some read it as proof that Pakistan was a religious project from the start. Others saw an old quarrel in new clothes.
- `17:42` And the Muslims of the United Provinces? Some left for Pakistan. Most stayed. Their story, on both sides of the border, is our final episode.

_[17:52] Scene `now-world`: void / blood; on screen: cite, samap, typer._

- `17:52` Beyond South Asia, the book raises a universal question. What happens when a nation is founded on a promise of faith, and asked to be a refuge, a utopia and a great power all at once?
- `18:04` The historian Faisal Devji has compared Pakistan with Israel, another state born at almost the same moment as a homeland for a religious community.
- `18:15` Others since, from Iran's revolution in 1979 to the self-declared caliphate of 2014, have promised to restore a lost golden polity. That comparison is ours, not the book's. But the 1940s show how such promises are made, and how hard they are to keep.

_[18:34] Scene `close`: room / archive; on screen: cite, cquote, seance._

- `18:34` Pakistan, Dhulipala concludes, was 'not insufficiently imagined, but plentifully and with ambition'.
- `18:41` If he is right, its troubles came not from too little vision, but from the distance between that vision and what any state could deliver.
- `18:49` In a dark room in 1955, a cigarette burned down on its wire stand. 'It is easier to acquire a country,' the spirit had said. 'It is extremely difficult to retain it.'
- `19:02` *(music beat, 3.4s)*

_[19:05] Scene `next`: void / ink; on screen: typer._

- `19:05` Next time: 1937. A new constitution, an election in the United Provinces, a Muslim leadership divided, and a partnership with Congress that never came to be.
