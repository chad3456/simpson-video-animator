## [0:56] Part 1: Forbidden Knowledge


_[1:00] Scene `fk-folder`: studio / teal; on screen: card, crowd, icon, label._

- `1:00` Every society has questions it would rather not ask. Saad calls the answers forbidden knowledge.
- `1:07` Some of it is ancient. Spinoza was cast out of his community for his ideas about God. Galileo spent his last years under house arrest.
- `1:16` Saad's claim is that today's taboos are guarded by a newer emotion. Not fear of heresy, but fear of causing harm.

_[1:24] Scene `fk-dominoes`: studio / midnight; on screen: dominoes, label, text._

- `1:24` The logic goes like this. If a finding could be misused, and misuse would hurt people, then the finding itself must be harmful.
- `1:33` Saad calls this faulty consequentialism. You judge a fact by what someone might do with it, not by whether it's true.
- `1:41` And once that rule is in place, almost anything can be ruled out of bounds.

## [1:47] Part 2: The Case Files


_[1:50] Scene `cf-fryer`: studio / ember; on screen: card, icon, label, particles._

- `1:50` Saad's first case is the Harvard economist Roland Fryer, who in 2016 studied how police use force.
- `1:59` He found racial gaps in lower-level force, like handcuffing and pushing people to the ground. But in the data he examined, he found no racial gap in police shootings.
- `2:10` That second result was not what many people wanted to hear, and Fryer has said he received threats.
- `2:17` In 2019, Harvard suspended him. The university said it was over complaints about his conduct toward staff, and that's the official record. Saad believes his research made him a target. Both accounts are public, so you can weigh them yourself.

_[2:33] Scene `cf-board`: city / midnight; on screen: crowd, icon, label, quote._

- `2:33` Another case is a US government body, the Disinformation Governance Board, announced in 2022 to counter false information.
- `2:43` Critics worried about a government deciding what's true. Within weeks it was paused, and it was later shut down.
- `2:50` Saad's point: censorship always arrives dressed as protection.

## [2:55] Part 3: Kind Words, Blurry Facts


_[2:59] Scene `words`: studio / plum; on screen: arrow, card, icon, label._

- `2:59` The same instinct reshapes language. Institutions keep swapping plain words for gentler ones.
- `3:05` A prisoner becomes a justice-involved individual. Obese becomes living in a larger body.
- `3:12` Supporters say words can wound, and dignity matters. Saad says a softer label can hide a hard reality, and you can't fix what you won't name.

_[3:22] Scene `health`: studio / teal; on screen: card, heart, label._

- `3:22` His sharpest example is the body positivity slogan, healthy at any size.
- `3:28` Fighting shame is reasonable. Shame doesn't help people get healthier, and stigma carries its own health costs.
- `3:35` But large studies link obesity to diabetes, heart disease, and several cancers. Kindness that skips that part, Saad argues, isn't kindness at all.

## [3:46] Part 4: Compassion Without Conditions


_[3:50] Scene `street`: city / ember; on screen: crowd, icon, label, text._

- `3:50` Saad's next case is homelessness in big American cities, where tents line some streets for blocks.
- `3:57` He borrows a phrase that the writer Michael Shellenberger applied to it: pathological altruism. Help that feels generous, but enables addiction and disorder.
- `4:08` Supporters of Housing First, the policy of giving people homes without preconditions, point to studies showing that more people stay housed.
- `4:17` Critics answer that housing alone does little for addiction or severe mental illness. Nearly everyone agrees that what many cities are doing now isn't working.

## [4:28] Part 5: Evil Is Not Make-Believe


_[4:32] Scene `fable`: land / dusk; on screen: critter, icon, label, shape._

- `4:32` There's an old fable. A scorpion asks a frog to carry it across a river. The frog says, you'll sting me. The scorpion says, why would I? We'd both drown.
- `4:43` Halfway across, it stings. As they sink, the frog asks why. And the scorpion says: it's my nature.

_[4:50] Scene `cyclists`: land / dusk; on screen: label, particles, pebble, quote, text._

- `4:50` Saad pairs the fable with a true story. In 2018, a young American couple cycling around the world wrote that evil was a make-believe concept, and that people are overwhelmingly kind.
- `5:03` Days later, in Tajikistan, they and two other cyclists were run down and killed by attackers who had pledged loyalty to the Islamic State.
- `5:13` Saad stresses that he isn't mocking them. His point is that assuming the best about everyone can be a fatal mistake, because the world doesn't always return the favour.

## [5:24] Part 6: The Case Against


_[5:28] Scene `against`: studio / midnight; on screen: label, scale._

- `5:28` Now, the other side.
- `5:30` Some caution about knowledge is wise. Scientists do withhold recipes for weapons, and research ethics exist for good reasons.
- `5:39` Language does change, and a new word isn't always censorship. Sometimes it's simply courtesy.
- `5:45` And Saad's own examples are disputed. Harvard says Fryer's case was about conduct, and Housing First has real evidence behind it.
- `5:55` Where both sides can meet: a fact doesn't become false because it's uncomfortable. You can be gentle in how you say something, without changing what's true.

- `6:05` Galileo was right, and the people he upset survived the news.
- `6:09` So the next time a fact feels too dangerous to say, ask three things. Is it true? Who is protected by hiding it? And who pays if we don't know?
