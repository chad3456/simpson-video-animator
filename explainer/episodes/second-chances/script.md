## [0:29] Part 1: The Revolving Door


_[0:33] Scene `recid`: studio / midnight; on screen: bars, icon, label._

- `0:33` Let's start with numbers. A large US Justice Department study followed prisoners released in 2005 from thirty states.
- `0:42` Within a year, about forty-four percent had been arrested again. Within three years, sixty-eight percent. Within nine years, eighty-three percent.

_[0:52] Scene `few`: studio / teal; on screen: dots, label._

- `0:52` Crime is concentrated. A small group of repeat offenders commits a large share of it. Saad cites survey data in which about one in eight male inmates said they'd been locked up ten or more times before.
- `1:05` So when a career offender is released yet again and commits another crime, Saad says, that's not bad luck. It's predictable.

_[1:14] Scene `defund`: city / ember; on screen: card, crowd, icon, label, text._

- `1:14` In 2020, after the murder of George Floyd, calls to defund the police spread across American cities.
- `1:22` Several cities cut police budgets, and some restored them as homicides rose. Researchers still debate how much policing changes explain that rise.
- `1:33` For Saad, it was empathy for offenders crowding out empathy for victims, who often live in the very same neighbourhoods.

## [1:41] Part 2: Heroes and Sneaks


_[1:44] Scene `hero`: land / dusk; on screen: book, label, particles, pebble._

- `1:44` Saad then makes an evolutionary argument. Across cultures, women tend to be drawn to men who protect, and romance novels are full of heroes who face down danger.
- `1:56` He suggests that some men now signal that same protectiveness, not through physical courage, but through loud displays of compassion.

_[2:05] Scene `sneak`: deep / plum; on screen: label, pebble._

- `2:05` Here he borrows a term from zoology: kleptogamy, which biologists once nicknamed, rather crudely, the sneaky eff-er strategy.
- `2:15` In some species, smaller males can't win fights with the big dominant male.
- `2:20` In cuttlefish, for example, a small male can disguise himself with a female's markings,
- `2:27` slip past the big male, and mate on the sly.
- `2:30` Saad's provocative claim is that some performative male allies are running a social version of the same strategy. It's a joke with a point, and plenty of people will find it unfair.

## [2:42] Part 3: The Subway Car


_[2:46] Scene `subway`: studio / midnight; on screen: crowd, label, shape, text._

- `2:46` In May 2023, on a New York subway train, a man named Jordan Neely, who was homeless and had a history of mental illness, shouted threats at passengers.
- `2:58` A former marine, Daniel Penny, restrained him in a chokehold for several minutes, with help from other riders. Neely died.
- `3:07` Penny was charged, and in December 2024 a jury acquitted him.

_[3:12] Scene `two-views`: studio / plum; on screen: card, label._

- `3:12` To some, Penny was a hero who protected frightened passengers. To others, a man died who needed help, not a chokehold.
- `3:21` Saad sees the outrage against Penny as empathy for an aggressor outranking empathy for the people he threatened. His critics see a death that should never have happened. Both views were argued in court.

## [3:34] Part 4: When Mercy Fails Victims


_[3:38] Scene `freiburg`: deep / midnight; on screen: label, particles, shape, text._

- `3:38` The hardest part of this chapter is about sexual violence.
- `3:42` In 2016, in Freiburg, Germany, a nineteen-year-old medical student named Maria Ladenburger, who volunteered with refugees, was raped and murdered on her way home from a party.
- `3:56` Her killer was an asylum seeker from Afghanistan who had earlier been convicted in Greece of attacking a woman and throwing her off a cliff, and had been released early. A German court sentenced him to life in prison.
- `4:10` Her family asked mourners to give to refugee charities. Saad sees that as empathy that survives even this. Many others see it as grace.

_[4:20] Scene `courts`: studio / ember; on screen: card, icon, label, quote._

- `4:20` Saad lists court cases in Britain and Austria where men convicted of sexual crimes against children received sentences he calls shockingly light, sometimes with their cultural background raised in mitigation.
- `4:34` And he cites Nadia Murad, a Yazidi woman enslaved and raped by ISIS, who won the Nobel Peace Prize. In 2021, a planned talk by her for Toronto students was called off, reportedly over worries it could stoke Islamophobia. After an outcry, the school board apologised.

## [4:55] Part 5: The Case Against


_[4:59] Scene `against`: studio / midnight; on screen: label, scale._

- `4:59` Critics raise strong points.
- `5:01` Many people who leave prison never commit a violent crime, and well-run programs, like education and job training behind bars, do reduce reoffending.
- `5:12` Individual horrors, however real, can't tell us about whole groups of migrants. Crime tracks age, sex, poverty and opportunity, as well as origin.
- `5:23` And mercy and due process protect the innocent too. The system that sometimes frees a guilty man also saves the wrongly accused.
- `5:32` Shared ground: victims deserve to come first, sentences should follow the evidence on who reoffends, and a second chance works best when it isn't the seventh.

_[5:43] Scene `outro`: city / dusk; on screen: house, label, pebble._

- `5:43` Second chances are one of the most humane ideas we have. Saad's question is whether we've stopped asking who pays when they fail.
