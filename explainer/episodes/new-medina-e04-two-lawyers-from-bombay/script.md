# LUMEN E4: "Two Lawyers from Bombay"

*In 1944 Jinnah told Gandhi to read a book on Pakistan written by Dr B. R. Ambedkar. Episode 4 follows the public argument that gave the Pakistan demand its maps, numbers and hostages, the League's answer, the failed talks on Malabar Hill, and the case against the idea that Pakistan was only a bargaining counter.*

## [0:00] Bombay. 17 September 1944. (A READING LIST)


_[0:05] Scene `letters`: room / archive; on screen: cite, doc, slate._

- `0:05` In September 1944 the two most powerful men in Indian politics were meeting, day after day, at Jinnah's house on Malabar Hill. Gandhi asked how Muslims, most of them descended from converts, could be a separate nation. Jinnah replied with a reading list.
- `0:23` Gandhi read the first book within two days. Ably written, he wrote back, but it had carried no conviction to him.
- `0:31` That book was not by a Muslim, and not by a Leaguer. It was by the man who would one day draft India's constitution: Dr Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar.

## [0:51] Chapter One: A Vague Idea?


_[0:55] Scene `vague`: void / ink; on screen: cite, cquote, tome, typer._

- `0:55` In March 1940, at Lahore, the Muslim League resolved that the Muslim-majority areas of the north-west and east should be grouped into independent states. That much historians agree on. Almost nothing after it.
- `1:10` For forty years after independence, the story was simple: Jinnah wanted Pakistan, and Jinnah got it. Then, in 1985, the historian Ayesha Jalal turned it upside down. Pakistan, she argued, was a bargaining counter: a deliberately vague demand that Jinnah used to win Muslims equality in a united India. Its followers imagined it in a host of shapes and forms, most of them vague.
- `1:34` Dhulipala's reply in this chapter is simple. Whatever happened at the negotiating table, out in public, from 1940 on, Pakistan was argued over in remarkable detail. And no one did more to give that argument shape than a second constitutional lawyer from Bombay.

## [1:52] Chapter Two: Thoughts on Pakistan


_[1:57] Scene `amb`: void / ink; on screen: cite, dossier, tome, typer._

- `1:57` Ambedkar was no friend of Congress, and no friend of Gandhi. He was the leader of India's untouchables, and he trusted neither caste Hindus nor the League to speak for his people.
- `2:09` While Congress answered Lahore with outrage, Ambedkar sat down and wrote. Within about four months he had a four-hundred-page study, with fourteen appendices of figures and three coloured maps showing where the new borders could run.
- `2:23` Those who fought Pakistan with similes and metaphors, he wrote, should remember that nonsense is no less nonsense because it rhymes. His own aim was to explain, not to convert. He had, he said, an open mind, though not an empty mind.

_[2:38] Scene `nation`: void / smoke; on screen: cite, cquote, typer._

- `2:38` His first question was the only one, he said, that really mattered: were India's Muslims a nation? Following the French thinker Ernest Renan, he dismissed race, language and territory. Nationality was a feeling.
- `2:52` A longing to belong to one's own group, he wrote, and a longing not to belong to any other. By that test, he concluded, Muslims had become a nation, and the map had handed them a homeland. The case for Pakistan took him only a few pages. The rest of the book was addressed to Hindus.

## [3:09] Chapter Three: A Safe Army, Not a Safe Border


_[3:14] Scene `money`: void / ink; on screen: cite, cquote, stat._

- `3:14` Ambedkar told Hindus they would lose nothing they needed. By his figures, Hindustan would keep about a hundred and twenty crore rupees of revenue. The two Pakistan states would have about thirty-six.
- `3:27` Then came his most notorious argument. The Indian army, he pointed out, recruited heavily from the Muslims of Punjab and the Frontier. In a war with a Muslim neighbour, he doubted, in language that is hard to read today, that such an army could be relied upon. Better, he concluded, a safe army than a safe border. Years later he admitted these fears might be imaginary.

_[3:49] Scene `ambmap`: void / ink; on screen: cite, cquote, samap, typer._

- `3:49` His maps did something no politician had yet dared. Pakistan on the existing provincial borders, he warned, would trap millions of Hindus and Sikhs inside a Muslim state. So Punjab and Bengal should themselves be divided, leaving their Hindu-majority districts in India. Seven years before it happened, he drew partition close to how it would fall.
- `4:10` He also named the logic behind the League's provinces: Muslim majorities in some provinces would hold their Hindu minorities as a guarantee for Muslim minorities elsewhere. He called it, with contempt, a scheme of communal peace through a system of communal hostages.

_[4:28] Scene `transfer`: void / smoke; on screen: cite, typer, verdict._

- `4:28` What of the people on the wrong side of the new lines? Greece, Turkey and Bulgaria had exchanged their minorities, Ambedkar noted. India could do the same, voluntarily and over an agreed period. The numbers, he wrote, were inconsiderable.
- `4:43` On the borders, he was right. Punjab and Bengal were divided in 1947, much as he had sketched.
- `4:51` On the people, he was catastrophically wrong. The transfer was neither small nor orderly. Perhaps fifteen million people were uprooted, and estimates of the dead run from two hundred thousand to two million. As Dhulipala notes, what Ambedkar did not foresee was the complete collapse of law and order.

## [5:09] Chapter Four: The League Answers


_[5:14] Scene `press`: void / ink; on screen: cite, paper, tome, typer._

- `5:14` Ambedkar's book set off a demand that Jinnah explain what he meant. So the League built a propaganda machine. Jinnah founded the English daily Dawn, and hired as its editor the legendary journalist Pothan Joseph, who said his new employer, though exacting, always inspired confidence.
- `5:32` Then came the League's direct answer to Ambedkar: two volumes of essays by a Punjabi journalist, M. S. Toosy, writing as MRT. Jinnah chose their titles, wrote their foreword, and had them published from his own house in Bombay.

_[5:48] Scene `geobody`: void / ink; on screen: cite, panel, samap, typer._

- `5:48` Where Ambedkar had hinted that Pakistan would be poor and weak, MRT insisted it would be strong: twice the size of France, as populous as Italy, with revenues greater than Turkey's or Egypt's, and the soldiers of the so-called martial races.
- `6:04` And something larger crept in. After the First World War, India's Muslims had campaigned to save the Ottoman Caliphate. Now League writers began to picture Pakistan as its successor: the leading power of the Islamic world. Jinnah publicly denied any pan-Islamic design. Yet one journalist recalled him at Iqbal's grave in 1942, saying that Pakistan held the key to the liberation of the entire Islamic world.

_[6:29] Scene `minority`: room / archive; on screen: cite, cquote, doc, typer._

- `6:29` Here the chapter lands its hardest blow on the idea of a bargaining counter. That idea assumes Jinnah would never abandon the Muslims of provinces like U.P. But in public he said, in effect, that they were not a nation at all. They were scattered, a sub-national group, entitled only to what any civilised government owed a minority.
- `6:50` Their protection would come from outside. If Muslims in Hindustan were oppressed, Jinnah asked, why should Pakistan not intervene, as Gladstone's Britain had for the Armenians? It was the hostage theory, now from the League's own side.
- `7:04` And again and again, he denied that Pakistan was a bargaining counter. It was, he said, a matter of life and death.

## [7:12] Chapter Five: Malabar Hill


_[7:17] Scene `talks`: void / ink; on screen: cite, dossier, typer._

- `7:17` The September talks turned on a formula drafted by the Congress leader C. Rajagopalachari. Muslim-majority districts would be marked out. All their inhabitants would vote on separation. And defence, foreign affairs and trade would stay common.
- `7:33` Gandhi wrote that he could find no parallel in history for a body of converts and their descendants claiming to be a nation apart from the parent stock.
- `7:42` Jinnah wanted whole provinces, and a vote of Muslims alone. The formula, he said, would leave Pakistan maimed and mutilated, the husk without the grain. Rajagopalachari answered that a new state should not begin by keeping people from the ballot box because of their religion.

_[8:00] Scene `third`: void / smoke; on screen: cite, cquote, typer._

- `8:00` The talks collapsed. Ambedkar turned his own phrase on the two men. They had not met with empty minds, he said, but neither had an open mind.
- `8:09` And he reminded both that there was a third party. Neither Gandhi nor Jinnah could carry India's untouchables into Hindustan or Pakistan without their consent, freely given, in a referendum of their own.

## [8:23] Chapter Six: The Philosopher of Pakistan


_[8:28] Scene `philosopher`: room / sepia; on screen: cite, cquote, tome, typer._

- `8:28` By the 1945 edition, Ambedkar sounded more doubtful. Pakistan, he now wrote, was unnecessary for Muslims of the majority provinces, and worse than useless for those left outside it. A mixed party of Hindus and Muslims would do more good. And yet, if Muslims insisted on Pakistan, it must be conceded.
- `8:48` Ten years later, after partition, he left no doubt. I was the philosopher, so to say, of Pakistan, he wrote, and he said he was glad India had been divided.

## [8:59] Chapter Seven: What the Historians Say


_[9:04] Scene `tests`: void / smoke; on screen: verdict._

- `9:04` So what holds up? That Ambedkar's book shaped the debate: well supported. Jinnah cited it, and Gandhi read it within two days.
- `9:12` That Pakistan was argued over in public, in detail, from 1940: supported, with caveats. The books, the maps and the figures exist. Jalal's point concerns the negotiating table, and the two claims can both be true.
- `9:28` That Jinnah wanted a sovereign state and never a bargaining counter: debated. His public words support Dhulipala. Jalal reads his private moves, above all his acceptance of the Cabinet Mission plan in 1946, quite differently.
- `9:43` And that League propaganda reveals Jinnah's real aims: contested. He wrote its foreword. But propaganda is what a party says in public, and critics say that is not the same thing as policy.

_[9:56] Scene `now`: void / blood; on screen: cite, cquote, typer._

- `9:56` The hostage theory did not die in 1947. In a footnote, Dhulipala recalls a story told by the Indian editor Shekhar Gupta. In 1993 the liberal Pakistani politician Salman Taseer asked him what would happen if Kashmir voted for Pakistan. Gupta said Indian Muslims might be victimised.
- `10:17` Taseer rolled up his sleeve, in mock anger, and asked whether Gupta thought Pakistan's Muslims would sit like cowards and do nothing. In 2011, Taseer was shot dead by his own bodyguard for opposing Pakistan's blasphemy law.
- `10:33` Minorities treated as hostages to a neighbour's behaviour: Ambedkar named the idea in 1940. Today it still shapes politics wherever a border divides kin. That last observation is ours.
